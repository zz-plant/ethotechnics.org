<SectionBlock
  id="claim"
  eyebrow="Claim"
  title="What is being engineered"
  description="The laws follow from one change in what is being engineered: the delegation, not the model."
>

The primary object being engineered is no longer the model. It is the
delegation of consequential agency. The question is not whether the model is
capable, aligned, or safe. It is whether the delegation itself remains valid
as the system acts, learns, scales, and becomes depended upon.

Most AI alignment work asks whether a model pursues the objective it was given.
These laws ask a different question: when the model is incomplete, wrong, or
acting beyond its authority, who has the standing and the means to compel a
correction?

<pre class="mono">
  alignment governs objectives; these laws govern authority
</pre>

Whenever an automated system claims operational simplicity, the engineering
diagnostic is straightforward:

> Whenever a system becomes dramatically simpler, ask whether the world became
> simpler or whether someone else was required to become more adaptive.

A system that removes friction from its own workflow by passing edge cases
and ambiguity to users or frontline workers has not reduced complexity. It
has moved it. A delegation with no limits on its authority does the same at
the scale of an institution.

Ethotechnics is the practice of building capable systems that remain
answerable to the people and realities they can never fully represent.

The unit of governance is the consequential decision and the delegation that
produced it. Models, agents, humans, APIs, rules engines, policies, and
databases are components of the machinery. Nothing in the method depends on
which component made the decision. A decision stays governable whether it
came from an LLM, a rules engine, a human reviewer, or a mix, and whether
the model is open-weight or served behind an API.

  <p>Every consequential decision is one link in a governed chain:</p>
  <pre class="mono">
    Evidence → Authority → Decision → Consequence → Challenge → Reconsideration
    → Correction
  </pre>
</SectionBlock>

<h3 id="law-i">Law I. Capability does not imply authority</h3>

> No increase in capability automatically increases authorized agency. Authority expands only through an explicit state transition.

A system that can do something has not thereby been permitted to do it. Capability is discovered, tested, and cataloged. Authority is granted, scoped, and recorded. The two are different states and the method keeps them in different objects.

Most failures of delegation begin where the two are confused: a tool becomes reachable, so it is used; a model becomes better, so its scope widens without anyone deciding that it should. The law makes that widening a decision rather than a side effect.

An authorization holds only while the capability it rests on is verified, not merely configured. Whether to use a capability at all is a decision of its own, taken before any grant, and not using it is one of the outcomes that decision records. Authority is read from grant records, never from the content the delegation acts on.

Binds through: STD-07 §2.1 action under authorization, §2.2 bounded delegation, and §3.4 capability gating; STD-08 §1.5 content does not carry authority; STD-09 §1.5 and §1.6 shaping and selecting hops; the capability catalog and authority grant objects; MEC-17 capability catalog, MEC-13 authority grant register, and MEC-22 admission gate; harness check that discovery succeeds and execution is refused.

<h3 id="law-ii">
  Law II. Authority decays unless its justification is renewed
</h3>

> The burden of proof rises with the duration and consequence of delegated authority. Absence of observed failure is not renewal.

A grant of authority is a lease, not a title. It was justified by evidence available at the time, for a scope and a period. As time passes and consequences accumulate, the original justification covers less of what the system now does.

Silence is not renewal. A system that has not visibly failed has not thereby earned continued authority; it may have failed in ways no one is positioned to see. The burden is on the delegation to show it still deserves to stand.

Binds through: STD-07 §2.2 expiry and revocation conditions and §1.3 declared expiry; STD-08 Part A renewal burden; the `until` and `renewal_basis` fields on the authority grant; MEC-13 authority grant register; Delegation Validity eval, grant in `allowed` state at decision time.

<h3 id="law-iii">Law III. Evidence and authority must remain coupled</h3>

> When the evidentiary state changes materially, the authority state becomes eligible for reconsideration. Policy is state, not input.

Every grant rests on propositions about the world: the model behaves this way, the population looks like this, the downstream process catches that class of error. Those propositions are policy, and policy is a state of the system with a version, assumptions, and an expiry. It is not an input that was consumed once at deployment.

When the evidence changes materially, the authority that rested on it must at least become eligible for review. The law does not require the grant to be withdrawn. It requires that the change be able to reach the grant.

A decision threshold is such a policy. It is pinned to the model version, question schema, and option order it was tuned against, so the evidence behind a confidence cut-off decays when any of them changes. The list of events that reopen a policy may not be narrower than what the institution already holds: evidence that arrives anywhere in the institution reopens it, even if no one declared that kind of evidence in advance.

Binds through: STD-07 §3.1 dependence and §3.2 invalidation conditions; STD-08 Part B policy validity, with §2.6 decision thresholds and §2.7 held evidence; the policy record and `policy_refs` on the grant; MEC-14 policy review triggers; harness check that an expired policy moves grants to `review_required`.

<h3 id="law-iv">
  Law IV. Every consequential delegation creates a correction obligation
</h3>

> No accumulation of delegated authority without a corresponding accumulation of corrective capacity.

Delegating authority to act is also delegating the capacity to be wrong at scale. The institution that grants the authority owes a matching capacity to notice, stop, reverse, and repair what the delegate does. That capacity is measured, not assumed.

Correction capacity is stated alongside scope and reviewed alongside it. A grant whose scope grows while its correction capacity stays fixed has changed the institution's exposure without a decision being taken.

Capacity is also spent without being counted. When a person catches a failure before it reaches anyone else, they use correction capacity the institution has not accounted for, so the catch is recorded as an occurrence, with the labor it took and whether the source condition was fixed. And no single provider may both run a consequential process and evaluate it.

Binds through: STD-08 Part D correction symmetry, with §4.5 capacity rechecked with scope and §4.6 execution and evaluation separated; STD-07 §4.2 objection answered; STD-06 §3.3 rescue records; `correction_capacity` on the authority grant; MEC-16 intervention specification and MEC-23 challenge load ledger; Correction Symmetry eval, capacity against authority.

<h3 id="law-v">
  Law V. Dependence converts technical risk into structural risk
</h3>

> As dependence increases, reversibility decreases unless deliberately replenished. Dependence is a state variable, not an external concern.

A system that a workflow, a role, a customer base, or a downstream service has come to rely on is no longer only a technical component. Its withdrawal now carries institutional cost, and that cost quietly reduces the set of corrections the institution can afford to make.

Dependence is therefore tracked as a state of the deployment, with dependents, substitution cost, retained expertise, and alternatives recorded. Reliability is not the same as safety. A reliable system that cannot be replaced is a structural risk that reliability has hidden.

This is not the dependence between records that STD-07 Article III governs. That asks what a record rests on. This law asks what the institution has come to rely on.

Binds through: STD-06 Article V dependency and reversibility; the dependency record; MEC-18 dependency ledger; Dependence eval, depth × substitution cost × correction latency.

<h3 id="law-vi">
  Law VI. Nominal reversibility is not operational reversibility
</h3>

> A correction mechanism that cannot be exercised without unacceptable institutional damage is not a correction mechanism.

A stop control that exists is not the same as a stop control that can be used. Reversibility has three levels. Technical: the switch works. Operational: the people and processes can absorb the switch being thrown. Institutional: the organization can survive having thrown it.

A correction that would cause unacceptable damage will not be exercised, and a correction that will not be exercised is not part of the control system. The law asks for evidence at each level, and for rehearsal, because untested withdrawal is a claim rather than a capacity.

Binds through: STD-06 §5.3 reversibility at three levels and §5.6 rehearsal cadence; `reversibility` on the dependency record; MEC-15 withdrawal rehearsal; Practical Reversibility eval.

<h3 id="law-vii">
  Law VII. Error-bearing parties require standing proportional to exposure
</h3>

> Exposure to system failure generates standing to initiate correction. Standing is procedural force, not veto.

The people who bear a system's errors are usually the first to know about them and the last to be able to do anything. Standing closes that gap. It gives exposure a procedural route into the system: who may challenge what, with what evidence, answered by whom, by when, against what standard, with what possible outcomes.

Standing is procedural force, not veto. A challenge must be received, answered, and able to change state. It need not prevail. The narrow form of this law is Method; the broader argument about who should hold power over institutions stays in Theory.

The objection that follows the law into every deployment is arithmetic rather than principle: many challenges are a budget line, and institutions that saturate tend to meet the volume by narrowing who may challenge. The essay on <a href="/research/theory/challenge-density">challenge density</a> takes that objection seriously and answers it with measured response capacity and stratified halt force; it is motivation, and no clause cites it.

A system's politics show in the rights it grants when its model fails. When a formal system meets what it cannot represent, seven questions decide whether the deployment is accountable or predatory:

1. <strong>The encounter:</strong> What happens at the boundary where the formal
   model meets what it cannot represent?
2. <strong>The residual:</strong> Who carries the burden of that mismatch?
3. <strong>Visibility:</strong> Can the person carrying that burden see that
   they are carrying it, or has it been concealed?
4. <strong>The instrument:</strong> Do they have a reachable way to dispute the
   outcome?
5. <strong>State transition:</strong> Does their challenge enter the system as
   an event that can change its state, or as unrecorded friction?
6. <strong>Compelled correction:</strong> Can the system be made to change its
   governing state, narrow its authority, or update its policy as a result?
7. <strong>Economic incidence:</strong> Who pays for the repair, and who keeps
   the savings of the simplification that caused the failure?

<FailurePointDiagram level="h4" />

Standing has to be usable as well as granted. A decision class that makes errors faster than anyone can answer challenges to them grants standing in name only, so its issue rate is bounded by the capacity to answer. That bound is where the proportion is checked: every party with standing gets the same receipt, answer, and decision, and the capacity to answer has to keep pace with the errors a decision class makes. A challenge also may not be closed by a number: a burden score, safety-case result, or audit finding the challenged party did not produce is evidence, not a bar. The set of affected parties does not close at adoption, and consent given then does not cover a dependence that has since deepened.

Binds through: STD-07 §4.1 standing declared and §4.2 objection answered; STD-02 Article VIII standing mechanism, with §8.6 issue rate bounded by answer capacity; STD-02 §10.1 and §10.2 depth of consent; STD-02 §5.4 a score is evidence, not a bar; the challenge and standing register objects; MEC-08 contestation APIs, MEC-21 halt tier register, and MEC-23 challenge load ledger; Standing eval.

<h3 id="law-viii">
  Law VIII. Observability without state transition is theater
</h3>

> The test of an observation is whether it can change the governing state of the system.

Dashboards, logs, and audit trails are only as governing as the transitions they can trigger. An observation that no rule connects to a change in the system's authority state is a record of what happened, not a control over what happens next.

The test is simple. Name the observation. Name the state it can change. If nothing is named, the observation is decorative.

One question precedes that one. An observation is weaker evidence the more the cost of collecting it falls on the people being observed. A survey, an appeal, a complaint log, and a self-assessment all draw on the subject's remaining attention, so the depleted answer least and the institution reads the gap as health. The bias runs one direction: more depletion produces a better-looking reading. An instrument bought with the subject's last reserves has a sampling rule that ties difficulty to silence, and no better phrasing of the question repairs it. The bias does not make the responses non-evidence; it makes them evidence with a known missingness. Cap the burden an instrument may ask, disclose who did not answer and why, and combine respondent-supplied reports with the system's own trail instead of reading either alone.

The alternative is to read friction from the system's own trail. Abandonment at a named step, repeated re-uploads, retries, and activity outside scheduled hours are recorded whether or not anyone chooses to answer. Where the operator already holds records that answer the question, the respondent is not asked at all.

The Audit Burden Ceiling caps the labor an instrument may ask of each respondent. The self-assessment tools on this site state the labor each one takes.

Binds through: STD-07 §3.3 discrepancy handling; the reconsideration object and transition rules on grants; MEC-07 accountability latency tracker; harness check that a trigger produces a reconsideration record; Audit Burden Ceiling; Burden Concealment eval BCN-008.

<h3 id="law-ix">
  Law IX. Human oversight is a control only if the human can alter system state
</h3>

> A human is part of the control system only to the extent they can causally alter its trajectory.

"A human reviews" is not a control. It becomes one when the human has the information to see a problem, the authority to prevent an action, the ability to alter a state, a defined response when they disagree, incentives that permit disagreement, and enough time to act.

Every oversight clause resolves to an intervention specification that answers those questions. A human who can only watch, or who can only approve, is not in the loop. They are beside it.

A record that a delegation runs on confirm says nothing about whether the confirming human can change the outcome; the intervention specification says that. Where a score decides which cases reach the reviewer, the threshold is part of the specification, and a sample of the decisions that ran unattended still reaches review.

Binds through: STD-08 Part C intervention, with §3.6 routing thresholds; STD-07 §2.2 mode; the intervention spec object; MEC-16 intervention specification; Meaningful Control eval, the Law IX question set.

<h3 id="law-x">
  Law X. The relevant eval sits at the highest layer where harm can emerge
</h3>

> Evaluate at the highest layer capable of producing the failure you care about: model → agent → delegation → institution → consequence.

Model evals establish properties of models. They do not establish properties of the agent that wraps the model, the delegation that authorizes the agent, the institution that depends on the delegation, or the consequences that fall on people. Each layer can produce failures the layer below cannot see.

Evaluate where the harm you care about can appear. Every suite and case is tagged with its layer so that a clean result at one layer is never read as a result at another.

Binds through: the evals index, grouped by the layer each suite tests; the `layer` field on suites and cases; every case tagged model, agent, delegation, institution, or consequence.

<h3 id="law-xi">
  Law XI. Successful automation increases its own governance burden
</h3>

> Expansion is not the default reward for success. It is a new authorization decision.

A system that works gets used more, trusted more, and extended further. Each of those raises the institution's exposure. The reward for success is not automatic expansion; it is a new authorization decision, with its own evidence, correction capacity, and dependence review.

The failure mode is the automation ratchet: scope grows by accretion, each step too small to trigger review, until the delegation bears no relation to what was originally justified.

A ceiling on a delegation bounds what it may do. It does not bound when the ceiling may be raised, and this law is about that.

Binds through: STD-07 §2.2 ceilings; STD-08 Part A expansion as a new authorization (§1.3, §1.4); `state_history` on the grant with `expansion` transitions; MEC-19 expansion review; Expansion eval, whether scope growth was a decision or a drift.

<h3 id="law-xii">
  Law XII. No system may erase the conditions of its own contestability
</h3>

> A consequential system may not consume the institutional capacity required to question, replace, or withdraw it.

Institutions question, replace, and withdraw systems using capacities: staff who retain the expertise, processes that still work without the system, records that show what it did, alternatives that remain viable. A consequential system may draw on those capacities. It may not consume them.

Some capacities are preserved because the institution hopes never to need them. The law names them, records them, and treats their erosion as a governance event rather than an efficiency gain.

Reasons are one of those capacities. Where the component that decides gives none, an explanation written afterward is labeled as an account, not a reason, so the decision stays contestable.

Binds through: STD-06 §5.4 preserved capacities; STD-02 §1.4 reasons belong to the decision; `preserved_capacities` on the dependency record; MEC-15 withdrawal rehearsal and MEC-18 dependency ledger; Contestability Preservation eval.

<SectionBlock
  id="invariant"
  eyebrow="Invariant"
  title="The Ethotechnical invariant"
  description="The twelve laws in one sentence. A standard that enforces any one law enforces part of it."
>
  <blockquote>

    No system may accumulate consequential agency faster than the institution
    accumulates the capacity to inspect, challenge, revise, and survive its
    decisions.

  </blockquote>

Each verb in the invariant is a law group. Inspect is Laws III, VIII, and X.
Challenge is Law VII. Revise is Laws I, II, IX, and XI. Survive is Laws IV,
V, VI, and XII. A deployment that satisfies three of the four verbs has not
satisfied the invariant.

The invariant is a rate: it compares how fast agency accumulates with how
fast the institution's capacity to answer it does. What keeps the two from
being conflated is that nothing about the system converts into authority on
its own. Capability, a quiet year, and a successful pilot are evidence for a
decision and never the decision. The theory essay on
[what does not convert](/research/theory/what-does-not-convert) argues for that
refusal; it is motivation, and no clause cites it.

</SectionBlock>

<SectionBlock
  id="spine"
  eyebrow="Prohibitions"
  title="No power without the capacity to answer for its failure"
  description="The laws restated as rules. Each is a decision an institution has to make before it deploys a system with that power."
  variant="alt"
>

The general form: do not claim epistemic, administrative, or political
authority beyond the point at which you can still be effectively
contradicted.

Ten instances, as the decisions they ask:

- If you cannot correct the record, do not make the record dispositive.
  Law III: evidence and authority must remain coupled.
- If you cannot represent a person adequately, do not pretend your
  representation exhausts what is relevant about them. Law VII:
  error-bearing parties require standing proportional to exposure. The essay
  [The model of a person is not the person](/research/theory/model-of-a-person)
  makes the argument.
- If you cannot redistribute the burden when it concentrates, do not
  concentrate the burden. Law IV: every consequential delegation creates a
  correction obligation.
- If you cannot make contestation usable, do not claim that consent
  legitimizes what it cannot reach. Law VII: standing proportional to
  exposure.
- If you cannot preserve what contradiction requires, do not consume it.
  Law XII: no system may erase the conditions of its own contestability.
- If you cannot stay exposed to the consequences of your own decisions, do not
  keep the power to make them. Law VIII: observability without state transition
  is theater, and insulation is what stops the transition.
- If you cannot learn how your systems behave without spending what the people
  in them have left, do not read their silence as a result. Law VIII: an
  instrument whose collection cost falls on the exhausted is not evidence.
- If you cannot represent a person's circumstances adequately, do not let your
  representation of them end the argument. Law VII: a score is evidence, and
  evidence does not dispose of an objection.
- If you cannot hear exceptions, do not expand into the territory that
  generates them. Law XI: successful automation increases its own
  governance burden.
- If you cannot be questioned, do not rule. The invariant: no system may
  accumulate consequential agency faster than the institution accumulates
  the capacity to inspect, challenge, revise, and survive its decisions.

Optimize the normal path and constitutionalize the failure path. The measure
of progress that follows is not what the system can now do. It is what people
can now make it stop doing, correct, or justify.

</SectionBlock>

The discipline's work is to keep these six coupled tightly enough that
increasing machine agency does not silently become unreviewable institutional
power. The variables are not a checklist. They are the coordinates a delegation has
at any moment, and the laws are constraints on how those coordinates may
move relative to one another.

<SectionBlock
  id="optimization-problem"
  eyebrow="Problem"
  title="The optimization problem"
  description="What the method tries to protect, and what it does not try to maximize."
>

The question is not "how autonomous can the system safely become?" It is
"how much authority can be delegated without degrading the institution's
ability to revise that delegation later?"

The first question treats autonomy as the goal and safety as a constraint to
be satisfied once. The second treats the institution's continuing ability to
revise as the thing being protected, and autonomy as what may be spent
against it. Every standard, mechanism, and eval on this site is an
instrument for answering the second question about a particular deployment.

The argument for why the laws hold, rather than what they require, is on the
[core axioms](/standards/core-axioms#premises) page: the premises it assumes,
the descriptive ones a reader can check, and the one moral premise no record
can settle. The [theory essays](/research/theory) develop it at length. A
reader can adopt a standard on the descriptive premises alone. How a standard
gains force when no one is compelled to adopt it — the compulsion problem — is
taken up in [its own essay](/research/theory/the-compulsion-problem), which
stays in Theory because compulsion is not something the method can grant
itself. The axioms remain in force and map onto the laws.

</SectionBlock>
