<SectionBlock
  id="overview"
  eyebrow="Overview"
  title="What it is"
  description="A management-system standard for establishing and improving AI governance policies and processes."
>

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies how organizations set up, implement, and maintain an
AI management system, including roles, policies, objectives, and continual
improvement cycles.

</SectionBlock>

<SectionBlock
  id="critique"
  eyebrow="Ethotechnics critique"
  title="Where the standard falls short"
  variant="alt"
>
  <ul class="card__list">
    <li>
      <strong>It certifies management, not authority:</strong> An accredited
      body certifies the organization's AI management system, and top management
      reviews it at planned intervals (clause 9.3). Nothing in it gives one
      system's authority to act an expiry date, after which the system must stop
      unless that authority is renewed on evidence.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Continuous improvement can normalize slow harm:</strong> “We’re
      improving” can coexist with multi-quarter remediation cycles in
      high-stakes contexts.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Artifacts over outcomes:</strong> Documentation, training, and
      lifecycle governance are easier to show an auditor than a record that a
      challenge changed a rule, or a measure of what the system costs people.
    </li>
  </ul>
</SectionBlock>

<SectionBlock
  id="failure-mode"
  eyebrow="Failure mode"
  title="Certified responsibility + unresolved time-in-harm"
  description="Well-managed organizations can still leave users stuck in harm windows."
>

The failure mode is a system that passes audits while users still cannot
compel reversal on a clock.

</SectionBlock>
