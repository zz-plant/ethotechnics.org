      <section id="articles" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article I: The right to notice and reasons</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          A person subject to an automated decision gets the reasons for it,
          in plain language, when the decision is made.
        </p>
        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§1.1</span>
            Decision objects: Every automated decision must produce a decision object containing reason codes, evidence sources, and scope boundaries.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§1.2</span>
            Plain language: Reasons must be written in language a layperson can understand. Legalese and internal codes are insufficient.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§1.3</span>
            Immediate availability: Reasons must be available at the moment of decision. An explanation deferred until someone asks for it does not comply.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§1.4</span>
            Reasons belong to the decision: A reason given under §1.1 and §1.2
            must state what the decision actually rested on: the inputs, rule,
            policy, and threshold that produced it. An explanation written
            afterwards by a component that did not make the decision is an
            account, not a reason. It must be labeled as one, and it does not
            satisfy §1.1 or §1.2 on its own. Where the component that decided
            gives no reasons, as a classifier that returns only a score does
            not, the decision object says so and names the policy, the
            threshold, and the inputs the decision ran on. The standing
            register entry for that class then includes whether the evidence
            supported the decision among its standards of review under §8.2.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article II: The right to an appeal path</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          The appeal starts from the decision itself and reaches someone who
          can reverse it.
        </p>
        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§2.1</span>
            Accessible intake: The appeal path must be reachable directly from the decision notification or interface.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§2.2</span>
            Named authority: The appeal path must identify the accountable authority with reversal power.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§2.3</span>
            Binding deadlines: Appeal deadlines must be published, time-bound, and enforced.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article III: The right to a review clock</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          Every review has a maximum duration, and this article fixes what
          happens to the person while it runs and when it runs out.
        </p>
        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§3.1</span>
            Declared maximum: Every review process must declare a maximum duration.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§3.2</span>
            Auto-escalation: When the clock expires without resolution, the case must auto-escalate to a higher authority or default to reversal.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§3.3</span>
            Interim protections: While review is pending, the affected party must receive provisional protection (continued access, status quo, safe fallback).
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article IV: The right to remedy and reversal</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          An upheld appeal reverses the decision. Wrongful harm is compensated
          without a separate claim, and a defect in a rule reaches everyone it
          affected.
        </p>
        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§4.1</span>
            Reversal as default: Upheld appeals result in reversal of the contested decision unless safety criteria explicitly prohibit it.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§4.2</span>
            Automatic compensation: Wrongful harm triggers automatic credits, fee waivers, or restitution without requiring a separate claim.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§4.3</span>
            Verified delivery: Remedy delivery is tracked with timestamps and verified by the affected party.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§4.4</span>
            Scope of remedy: Where a defect is found to arise from a rule, a model, or a notice rather than from one decision, the remedy reaches every affected case without a further claim. The institution enumerates the affected population from its own records, corrects those cases, and tells each person what was corrected. Remediation reachable only by individual appeal does not discharge the obligation.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article V: The right to evidence and accountability</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          Every decision leaves a receipt and every contested decision leaves
          an audit trail. Appeal outcomes are published. A score is evidence in
          a challenge, not the answer to it.
        </p>
        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§5.1</span>
            Decision receipts: Every decision emits a receipt with timestamp, owner, and decision object reference.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§5.2</span>
            Outcome transparency: Appeal passage rates and reversal rates are published and used to improve decisions.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§5.3</span>
            Audit trails: Audit trails link decisions to evidence, reviewers, and outcomes for every contested case.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§5.4</span>
            What a score may not do: A measurement of burden, a safety-case result, an audit finding, or any other score the challenged party did not produce is evidence and not a bar. It may not dispose of an objection, and it may not be cited to establish that a person's account is unfounded. Where a score and an account conflict, the conflict goes to a named reviewer holding authority to resolve it, and the score does not travel as the default. An institution that answers a challenge with a number has answered a different question.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section id="review-infrastructure" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article VI: The infrastructure of review</h2>

        A review can happen only if three things exist, and each is easy to
        omit: a human who can be asked, reviewers who are not penalized for
        finding in the challenger's favor, and records that last long enough,
        and resist tampering well enough, to support the investigation. A
        review offered without all three exists only on paper.

        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§6.1</span>
            Human review on request: Where an automated system denies a claim
            or takes a consequential action, a human review of that outcome is
            available on request. The request requires no new evidence, no
            additional form, and no stated reason, because the alternative is
            that the system's output becomes final by default.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§6.2</span>
            Reviewer incentives: Where reviewers are evaluated, the evaluation
            must not reward upheld denials, closure speed, or any other metric
            that biases against remedies. A reviewer whose performance depends
            on the answer the institution prefers is not a reviewer. The
            evaluation policy is itself evidence, and it is disclosed to the
            people whose cases depend on it.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§6.3</span>
            Tamper-evident retention: Where logs support review or redress,
            they are retained for at least the full review and appeal period
            plus the limitation window, and they are tamper-evident: a deletion
            or modification after a dispute begins is itself recorded. A log
            the operator can silently rewrite is not evidence; it is the
            operator's account.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section id="liability-and-capacity" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article VII: Liability and capacity</h2>

        Deploying automation does not redeploy responsibility. Two failures
        recur: the institution pointing at the vendor's infrastructure when
        access or remedy is needed, and the institution scaling the volume of
        decisions without scaling the capacity that must answer them. Both
        convert a right into a queue.

        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§7.1</span>
            Operator liability: Where automation causes an error or a delay,
            liability for the resulting harm rests with the operating
            institution. The delegation was the institution's, and the harm
            travels the same path as the benefit. That the system acted is a
            description of the mechanism, not an answer to the person harmed.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§7.2</span>
            Vendor-managed infrastructure: Where a vendor operates the logs,
            the models, or the controls, the operating institution still
            guarantees the affected person's access and remedy. A contract with
            a vendor is not visible to the person the system decided about and
            does not shorten their route. The obligation survives the
            outsourcing.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§7.3</span>
            Proportional review capacity: Where automation increases the
            throughput of decisions, review capacity is funded in proportion to
            the actions taken, and the capacity plan is part of the
            deployment's evidence. Throughput that outruns review converts the
            review right into a queue, and a queue is the mechanism by which
            this standard's clocks become decorative.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section id="standing-mechanism" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article VIII: The standing mechanism</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          "A channel that accepts a complaint is not standing. Standing is who
          may challenge what, answered by whom, by when, against what standard,
          with what possible outcomes."
        </p>

        Every formal model meets cases it cannot represent. When it does,
        someone carries the error. Without a route to challenge the decision,
        they carry it in silence. This article turns exposure to a system's
        errors into standing: the power to make a named responder review the
        decision by a deadline, against a published standard, with a published
        set of outcomes.

        Contestability requires seven things to be named together: who may
        challenge, what may be challenged, what evidence is admissible, who
        is obliged to respond, by what deadline, against what standard of
        review, and which state transitions a successful challenge can
        produce. Named separately, or named in a policy nobody can cite, they
        do not compose into a route.

        <a href="/standards/std-07-revisable-delegation-record">STD-07</a>
        §4.1 already requires a record that affects outsiders to declare who
        may object and where, and §4.2 already obliges an accepted objection
        to be answered with a revision or a reasoned refusal within the
        reversal clock. This article does not restate either. It adds the two
        fields those clauses leave open: the standard the responder decides
        against, and the set of state transitions a successful challenge can
        produce.

        <Std02RecourseDiagram />

        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.1</span>
            The register: For each class of consequential decision, the operator
            must publish a standing register entry naming
            <code>who_may_challenge</code>, <code>what_may_be_challenged</code>,
            <code>admissible_evidence</code>, <code>responder</code>,
            <code>response_deadline</code>, <code>standard_of_review</code>, and
            <code>possible_state_transitions</code>. An entry missing any of the
            seven is incomplete, and the decision class it covers is
            uncontestable in practice.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.2</span>
            The standard of review: The register must state what the responder
            decides against: whether the decision followed policy, whether the
            policy was correct, whether the evidence supported the decision, or
            whether the outcome was reasonable. A responder who is not told what
            to decide against will decide whether the process ran, which is the
            one question the challenger did not ask.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.3</span>
            Possible transitions: The register must enumerate the state changes a
            successful challenge can produce, drawn from the same set as a
            reconsideration outcome: the decision stands, is narrowed,
            suspended, reversed, corrected, or the grant behind it is expanded
            or withdrawn. A challenge route with no enumerated transition is
            correspondence, not recourse.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.4</span>
            Error-bearing parties: Standing extends to the parties who absorb the
            system's errors. That includes the people the decision falls on and
            the operators who handle its exceptions, work its queues, and
            perform the manual repair the automation assumes. Their standing is
            proportional to exposure: the more of the system's error a party
            absorbs, the stronger the procedural force their challenge carries.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.5</span>
            Force, not veto: Standing is procedural force. A party with standing
            can compel receipt, an answer within the deadline, and a decision
            against the published standard. They cannot compel the outcome. A
            challenge that must be answered but need not prevail is standing; a
            challenge that halts the system on assertion is a veto, and this
            standard does not require one.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§8.6</span>
            Issue rate bounded by answer capacity: The register entry must state
            how many challenges the responder can answer within
            <code>response_deadline</code> in a period. Where the class's issue
            rate in that period, multiplied by its measured error rate, or by an
            assumed rate stated in the entry until one is measured, exceeds
            that capacity, the class is uncontestable in practice under §8.1
            until capacity is raised or the issue rate is lowered. Narrowing who
            may challenge does not cure it. The bound is set on errors made,
            not on challenges received, because an error nobody could
            challenge in time never shows up as a challenge.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section id="prospective-auditability" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article IX: Prospective auditability</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          "Forensic records answer what happened. A governing record answers
          whether this should still be running."
        </p>

        Most audit trails are built for reconstruction after harm. They can
        establish what the system did and rarely establish whether the
        delegation behind it still deserves to stand. The difference is what
        is retained: an audit trail retains outputs, and a governing record
        retains the justification, the assumptions, and the correction route
        that were in force at the time.

        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§9.1</span>
            Retention for decision, not only reconstruction: Records must retain
            what is needed to decide whether the delegation still deserves to
            stand: the authority it ran under, the policies that authority
            rested on with their versions, the assumptions those policies
            declared, the correction capacity claimed at the time, and the
            challenges received and how they were answered.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§9.2</span>
            Answerable without the vendor: The retained set must be sufficient
            for the operating institution to answer the standing question
            without the cooperation of the supplier of any component. A
            justification that can only be assembled by the party whose product
            is under review is not evidence available to the review.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§9.3</span>
            Reviewed on a cadence: The standing question is asked on a published
            cadence, not only after an incident. The review produces a dated
            record whether or not anything changed, so that a period in which
            nobody asked is visible as such.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>

      <section id="depth-of-consent" class="standard-section">
        <h2>Article X: The depth of consent</h2>
        <p class="standard-article-quote">
          "Consent to enter a dependency is not consent to every degree of
          dependency its operation produces."
        </p>

        A party who adopts a system consents to the relationship as it stands
        at adoption. They cannot consent to the constraint set the system's own
        success later manufactures: data accumulates inside it, collaborators
        organize around it, the alternatives that were live at adoption stop
        being maintained, and the competence needed to work without it
        atrophies. Each of those changes the terms without anyone deciding to
        change them. By the time they have all happened, "you chose this" no
        longer describes the present relationship, and it is the present
        relationship the party is living under.

        Article VIII establishes who may challenge and with what force. This
        article addresses when that set changes and when a recorded consent
        stops covering what the system now does. The measurements it relies on
        are the dependency record's, held under{" "}
        <a href="/standards/std-06-human-impact-safety-case">STD-06</a> Article
        V.

        <ol class="standard-clauses">
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§10.1</span>
            The affected set does not close at adoption: Standing MUST extend to
            parties who became affected after adoption, including those who never
            consented and those whose exposure changed materially without any new
            decision by them. Enumerating the affected parties at adoption
            records who was affected then; it does not define who may challenge
            now. A standing register entry that admits only the parties named at
            adoption is incomplete under §8.1.
          </li>
          <li>
            <span class="standard-clause-id">§10.2</span>
            Deepened dependence reopens consent: Where dependence has materially
            deepened since consent was given, the operator MUST disclose the
            change to the consenting party and MUST NOT treat the original
            consent as covering the new degree. Deepening is material when, as
            against the values recorded when consent was given, any level of
            <code>reversibility</code> has moved from evidenced to not evidenced,
            or <code>exposure_score</code> or <code>substitution_cost</code> has
            risen by a quarter or more. Disclosure is owed within 30 days of the
            dependency record entry that shows the move, states which measure
            moved and by how much, and names the standing route under §8.1. Until
            it is made, the operator MUST NOT cite the original consent as the
            basis for the current degree of dependence.
          </li>
        </ol>
      </section>
