      <strong>Axiom 2 — Consent:</strong> Legitimate systems secure informed

      responsibility for the outcomes and latency of their automation.
    </li>

      <strong>Axiom 4 — Reversibility:</strong> High-impact actions require safe
      rollback paths and transparent appeal channels.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 5 — Legibility:</strong> Users must be able to see,
      understand, and audit the burden imposed on them.
    </li>
  </ol>

> A formal system does not eliminate what it cannot represent; it redistributes
> the burden of dealing with it. The political question is who is forced to carry
> that residual, and with what power to contest or correct the system.

<pre class="mono">
formal simplification → residual complexity → burden allocation → politics
</pre>

<BurdenDerivationDiagram />

The dispute with technocratic rationalism is not that models are inherently flawed
or that quantification is dehumanizing. It is that <strong>model incompleteness has
distributive consequences</strong>. A formal model leaves reality out; an institution
nevertheless acts on the model; the omitted reality eventually collides with that
action; and someone must absorb the mismatch.

Under the technocratic fiction of <em>closure without remainder</em>, what is omitted
from the formal system is treated as eliminated from reality. An institution measures
its internal computational latency in milliseconds while treating the hours of human
attention, administrative appeals, and workaround labor spent outside its boundary as
free, zero-cost externalities. This is the <strong>falsified denominator</strong>:
evaluating efficiency strictly within the model boundary while treating the human
environment as an infinite, unbudgeted shock absorber.

Those who absorb this residual mismatch are not incidental to the system. <strong>They
are part of its true operating architecture.</strong> If users must contort themselves
around software, if frontline workers manually patch pipeline edge cases, or if
citizens must navigate bureaucratic labyrinths to correct automated errors, that
friction is the system functioning as designed: running lean internally by extracting
corrective labor from the surrounding human world.

A model evaluation establishes properties of a model. It cannot establish
the alignment of the system that model is deployed into, because that system
includes the people who notice what the model got wrong, interpret what it
left ambiguous, and absorb what it broke. Where those corrections are
routine rather than exceptional, the people making them are not users of the
safety architecture. They are part of it.

That produces a specific asymmetry, and it is the reason these axioms are
entitlements rather than preferences. An institution that depends on a
population's corrective capacity while withholding corrective authority has
delegated a safety function without delegating the power to exercise it. It
asks for judgment and refuses the standing that judgment implies.

The claim is narrower than a general argument for participation, and the
narrowness is what makes it hold. Ordinary productive labor makes a system
valuable; extracting it without a say is a distributive wrong. Corrective
labor does something further: [absorbing a failure
competently](/glossary/extraction-by-endurance) removes the evidence that it
occurred. The metric improves <em>because</em>
someone caught it. So the institution loses not only the labor but its own
capacity to know how the system is performing — a{" "}

<a href="/glossary/fail-silent">fail-silent</a> state in which the absorbing
humans are the mechanism. Dependence on correction is therefore self-concealing
in a way dependence on production is not, and a clean dashboard cannot be read
as evidence of health until absorption has been measured separately.

Each axiom follows from refusing that extraction:

- <strong>Finitude (Axiom 1)</strong> refuses the falsified denominator. Human
  attention is finite, non-renewable biological capacity. A system that budgets
  zero time for the human labor required to navigate its omissions is running on
  uncounted absorption.
- <strong>Consent (Axiom 2)</strong> and <strong>Reversibility (Axiom 4)</strong>
  hold because a correction that cannot be exercised is a correction only in
  name, and a handoff without the power to exit or rollback shifts the risk of
  model failure entirely onto the dependent party.
- <strong>Stewardship (Axiom 3)</strong> enforces the moral principle of
  delegated intelligence: <em>whoever receives the benefits of formal
  simplification should not be permitted to externalize the costs of whatever
  the simplification leaves unresolved.</em> The boundary of a model is never the
  boundary of institutional responsibility. Responsibility that is delegated
  downward while authority stays upward is not stewardship at all. Stated as a
  slogan: <em>the burden should run uphill.</em> The
  [casebook](/casebook) is what this looks like when it fails: in Robodebt the
  burden of proof ran downhill, onto recipients, for three years. An institution
  that simplifies reality for its own operational advantage presumptively bears
  the cost when the simplification fails.
- <strong>Legibility (Axiom 5)</strong> holds because burden that cannot be
  seen cannot be governed, and corrective burden is the kind that hides by
  default. Undocumented residual complexity cannot be held to account.

This bounds the claim as well as grounding it. Authority follows
demonstrated dependence, so it is proportional rather than unlimited, and it
needs an instrument: the{" "}

<a href="/evals/burden-concealment">Burden Concealment evals</a> measure how far
reported performance depends on absorption that is not being counted. It also
has a failure mode worth naming, because standing that tracks
<em>current</em> indispensability rewards staying indispensable. Standing should
attach to dependence that has been documented, and survive the dependence being
engineered away — otherwise the doctrine preserves the fragility it exists to
expose.

Dependence also runs the other way, and the argument holds in that direction
too. Where a population depends on the institution's system and cannot leave
it at acceptable cost, the ordinary inference is that the institution may
direct it. The axioms refuse that inference. A party who cannot exit bears
every error the system makes for as long as it makes it, so the less a
population can leave, the more of the institution's correction capacity is
the only correction there is, and the more the institution owes.

<strong>Consent</strong> and <strong>Reversibility</strong> are therefore
strongest exactly where they are least likely to be demanded, and the measure
that scales them is <a href="/glossary/exit-cost">exit cost</a>, taken from the
dependent party's side. Neither direction of dependence licenses command; both
raise what is owed. The theory essays on [dependence without
standing](/research/theory/dependence-without-standing) and [dependence running
both ways](/research/theory/dependence-runs-both-ways) carry the two arguments;
the axioms above are what they are arguments for.
