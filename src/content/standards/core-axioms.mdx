<SectionBlock
  id="axioms"
  eyebrow="Axioms"
  title="The axioms of accountable systems"
  description="A doctrinal checklist for authoring or auditing standards."
>
  <ol class="axiom-list">
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 1 — Finitude:</strong> A person's time, attention, money,
      and stamina are finite. A system must budget what it spends of them as it
      budgets its own resources.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 2 — Consent:</strong> Agreement counts as consent only if
      the person could understand it, refuse it without penalty, and withdraw it
      later. Compliance under dependence is not consent.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 3 — Stewardship:</strong> The party that benefits from
      automation answers for its outcomes and its delays. It cannot pass that
      responsibility to an operator who lacks the authority to override.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 4 — Reversibility:</strong> High-impact actions require a
      rollback path and an appeal that work in practice, at a cost the affected
      person can bear.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Axiom 5 — Legibility:</strong> An institution must record the
      burden its system imposes and show that record to the people carrying it.
      Producing the record is the institution's work, not theirs.
    </li>
  </ol>
  <p class="small muted">
    Restated in v2.0.0 (October 5, 2026) so each axiom says what the derivation
    below argues for. The five names and their mapping to the laws did not
    change.
  </p>
</SectionBlock>

<SectionBlock
  id="premises"
  eyebrow="Premises"
  title="What the axioms assume"
  description="The starting points, stated so a reader can accept or reject them without reading the theory essays. The derivation below argues from them."
  variant="alt"
>

The argument starts from four claims about people. They are **finite**: their time, attention, money, and stamina run out. They
are **dependent**: on other people, and on institutions they cannot
leave at an acceptable cost. They are **heterogeneous**: no
category fits all of them, and some fit none. They are
**adaptive**: they bend to a system that does not bend to them, and
the bending does not appear in its records.

One principle follows from those claims. The work of adapting to an
arrangement should not fall systematically on one side, should not be kept out
of reach of the party able to reduce it, and should not use up the capacity the
people doing it need for anything else, including objecting.

The [principle of non-expropriation of
resilience](/glossary/principle-of-non-expropriation-of-resilience) is its
sharpest case. A person's ability to adapt does not give an institution a claim
on that adaptation. That someone can work around a defect is not a reason they
should have to.

Each descriptive premise in the derivation can be checked against an
institution's own records, and some give the institution reasons of its own to
hold to the axioms: absorbed failures blind its instruments, and reliability
that rests on compensation is borrowed. Those reasons run out in one case. A
carer who never stops, or a claimant who gives up without complaint, can absorb
an arrangement's failures indefinitely. Nothing breaks, and the institution
loses nothing it measures. Only a moral premise covers that case:

> What people give up to stay included in an arrangement is a cost of that
> arrangement, whether or not the institution ever pays it.

This premise cannot be checked. It is the one the method asks a reader to
accept. A reader who rejects it can still adopt every standard on the
descriptive premises alone. What they give up is a reason to object to
extraction that works.

</SectionBlock>

<SectionBlock
  id="derivation"
  eyebrow="Derivation"
  title="Why these are owed, not recommended"
  description="The axioms are stated above as doctrine. This is the argument that grounds them, which decides whether an operator may treat them as good practice to be traded off."
>

> A formal system does not eliminate what it cannot represent; it redistributes
> the burden of dealing with it. The political question is who is forced to carry
> that residual, and with what power to contest or correct the system.

<pre class="mono">
  formal simplification → residual complexity → burden allocation → politics
</pre>

<BurdenDerivationDiagram />

The dispute with technocratic rationalism is not that models are inherently flawed
or that quantification is dehumanizing. It is that **model incompleteness has distributive consequences**. A formal model leaves reality out; an institution
nevertheless acts on the model; the omitted reality eventually collides with that
action; and someone must absorb the mismatch.

Under the technocratic fiction of _closure without remainder_, what is omitted
from the formal system is treated as eliminated from reality. An institution measures
its internal computational latency in milliseconds while treating the hours of human
attention, administrative appeals, and workaround labor spent outside its boundary as
free. This is the **falsified denominator**: efficiency measured
only inside the model boundary, while the people outside it are counted as
unlimited capacity that never appears in the budget.

Those who absorb this residual mismatch are not incidental to the system. **They are part of its true operating architecture.** If users must contort themselves
around software, if frontline workers manually patch pipeline edge cases, or if
citizens must navigate bureaucratic labyrinths to correct automated errors, that
friction is the system functioning as designed: running lean internally by extracting
corrective labor from the surrounding human world.

A model evaluation establishes properties of a model. It cannot establish
the alignment of the system that model is deployed into, because that system
includes the people who notice what the model got wrong, interpret what it
left ambiguous, and absorb what it broke. Where those corrections are
routine rather than exceptional, the people making them are not users of the
safety architecture. They are part of it.

That produces a specific asymmetry, and it is the reason these axioms are
entitlements rather than preferences. An institution that depends on a
population's corrective capacity while withholding corrective authority has
delegated a safety function without delegating the power to exercise it. It
asks for judgment and refuses the standing that judgment implies.

The claim is narrower than a general argument for participation, and the
narrowness is what makes it hold. Ordinary productive labor makes a system
valuable; extracting it without a say is a distributive wrong. Corrective
labor does something further: [absorbing a failure
competently](/glossary/extraction-by-endurance) removes the evidence that it
occurred. The metric improves _because_
someone caught it. So the institution loses not only the labor but its own
capacity to know how the system is performing — a [fail-silent](/glossary/fail-silent) state in which the absorbing
humans are the mechanism. Dependence on correction is therefore self-concealing
in a way dependence on production is not, and a clean dashboard cannot be read
as evidence of health until absorption has been measured separately.

Four descriptive premises sit under the five, and none of them is a sixth
axiom. Each is a claim about the world the axioms answer to, stated because
it is the reason the axioms are owed rather than preferred, and each is
checkable.

Where the burden goes is not random. Absorbed friction flows along a **gradient of least contractual resistance** and pools at the node
least able to refuse: the clinician held by licensure and duty of care, the
claimant whose benefits are the institution's to time, the moderator whose
replacement costs nothing. The routing is structural rather than intentional — a
system costs what its cheapest node will absorb, and a vendor whose failures
route to people who cannot bill for the fixing undercuts one whose failures land
on its own balance sheet. That is the descriptive ground under
**Stewardship**: burden runs downhill by default, so holding it
uphill takes a recorded obligation rather than goodwill, and it is why standing
tracks exposure rather than title. The premise is checkable the way the rest is:
the
[rescue register](/glossary/rescue-register) and the burden
dashboards record where absorption actually lands, and if it concentrates at the
most powerful nodes, the gradient is wrong.

The second premise is temporal. **An institution's horizon is unbounded and a person's is not.** A delayed decision costs the
institution a scheduling slot and costs the person part of the only life the
decision is about, so no queue is as neutral as its wait-time statistic.
That asymmetry is what lets waiting do the work of denial without a denial — [attrition as resolution](/glossary/attrition-as-resolution) — and it
is why the method's clocks are structural rather than procedural: the
[hard clock](/mechanisms/patterns/hard-clock), the expiry default,
and the statutory timer exist to give the person's clock a counterweight the
institution cannot outlast. The premise misdescribes its targets if delays turn
out to resolve independently of the claimant's endurance, and those clocks are
the test.

The third premise is about what counts as a response. **An architecture is defined by the state transitions it makes impossible.**
Not by its values documents, its dashboards, or the tone of its leadership.
What remains technically and legally possible is what the institution chose
to permit, and permission is a record — so the audit that matters compares
the values documents with the permission records rather than trusting either
alone. Call this **constraint realism**. It is the method's
reason for writing invariants instead of principles: a principle can be
signed and ignored, while a transition that cannot happen cannot be ignored,
and it is why observability without state transition is theater rather than
progress. This is the premise the standards layer stands on, and it is the
reason a code of conduct is never a deliverable here.

The fourth premise is about what a performance figure contains.
**Observed performance is designed capacity plus human compensation.**
The falsified denominator is the boundary error; this is the quantity it hides.
A deployment's [intrinsic performance](/glossary/intrinsic-performance) is what
the formal system achieves on its own. Its [compensated
performance](/glossary/compensated-performance) adds what people's workarounds,
rework, and off-hours repair supply on top, and reported figures are the sum.
That is the descriptive ground under **Finitude** and
**Legibility**: the compensation is drawn from finite capacity, and
nobody sees it unless someone records it. The [Burden Concealment evals](/evals/burden-concealment) test the
premise by measuring how much of a reported figure depends on absorption nobody
counts. If a deployment's figures hold when the compensating work stops, the
premise misdescribes it.

Each axiom follows from refusing that extraction:

- **Finitude (Axiom 1)** refuses the falsified denominator. A
  system that budgets zero time for the human labor required to navigate its
  omissions is running on uncounted absorption.
- **Consent (Axiom 2)** and
  **Reversibility (Axiom 4)**
  hold because a correction that cannot be exercised is a correction only in
  name, and a handoff without the power to exit or roll back shifts the risk of
  model failure entirely onto the dependent party. Together with Finitude, they
  enforce republican non-domination against arbitrary institutional
  architecture.
- **Stewardship (Axiom 3)** enforces the moral principle of
  delegated intelligence:
  _whoever receives the benefits of formal simplification should not be permitted to externalize the costs of whatever the simplification leaves unresolved._
  The boundary of a model is never the boundary of institutional responsibility.
  Responsibility that is delegated downward while authority stays upward is not
  stewardship at all. Stated as a slogan: _the burden should run uphill._
  Stewardship requires discretionary craft judgment with unpenalized override
  authority; treating a human operator without real override power as a
  liability sponge to absorb automated errors is counterfeit buffering. The
  [casebook](/casebook) is what this looks like when it fails: in Robodebt the
  burden of proof ran downhill, onto recipients, for three years. An institution
  that simplifies reality for its own operational advantage presumptively bears
  the cost when the simplification fails.
- **Legibility (Axiom 5)** holds because burden that cannot be seen
  cannot be governed, and corrective burden is the kind that hides by default.
  Undocumented residual complexity cannot be held to account. Asking the people
  carrying the burden to report it repeats the extraction, so the record has to
  come from the system's own trail of retries, abandonments, and off-hours work.

Together, the five axioms form the **tripartite invariant**:

_non-domination_ (relational independence from uncontrolled
architecture),
_burden accounting_ (tracking the finite biological capacity an
arrangement consumes), and _error-correcting authority_ (cybernetic
return paths ensuring that authority remains conditional on downstream
correction).

This bounds the claim as well as grounding it. Authority follows
demonstrated dependence, so it is proportional rather than unlimited, and the
evals that test the fourth premise are also how the dependence is
demonstrated. It also has a failure mode worth naming, because standing that tracks _current_ indispensability rewards staying indispensable. Standing should
attach to dependence that has been documented, and survive the dependence being
engineered away — otherwise the doctrine preserves the fragility it exists to
expose.

Dependence also runs the other way, and the argument holds in that direction
too. Where a population depends on the institution's system and cannot leave
it at acceptable cost, the ordinary inference is that the institution may
direct it. The axioms refuse that inference. A party who cannot exit bears
every error the system makes, for as long as it makes it. The less a
population can leave, the more the institution's own correction capacity is
the only correction that population has. The institution therefore owes
more, not less.

**Consent** and **Reversibility** are therefore
strongest exactly where they are least likely to be demanded, and the measure
that scales them is [exit cost](/glossary/exit-cost), taken from the
dependent party's side. Neither direction of dependence licenses command; both
raise what is owed. The theory essays on [dependence without
standing](/research/theory/dependence-without-standing) and [dependence running
both ways](/research/theory/dependence-runs-both-ways) carry the two arguments;
the axioms above are what they are arguments for.
