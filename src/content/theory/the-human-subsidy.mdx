When an automated decision system or administrative institution functions, conventional accounting attributes its performance to its formal architecture: staffing formulas, software pipelines, protocols, and budget.

That attribution is often an accounting fiction. Much of what registers as institutional capacity is actually borrowed from workers, patients, families, and citizens who quietly repair what the formal system leaves broken. The nurse catches the medication error. The employee reconstructs an undocumented process. The patient coordinates five uncommunicative specialists. The claimant translates contradictory bureaucratic notices.

This labor is not incidental friction. It is productive of institutional functionality. The formal system does not possess the capacity to produce its reported outcomes on its own. It survives on a continuous human subsidy.

## Analytical disembedding

In political economy, Karl Polanyi used "disembedding" to describe the historical separation of markets from social relations. Here, the term serves an analytical purpose: de-naturalizing what institutional accounting collapses together.

Conventional accounts fuse several distinct elements that must be pulled apart:

- **Performance from capacity.** An arrangement producing acceptable outcomes does not establish that the arrangement itself possesses the capacity to produce them. Part of that capacity is borrowed from informal human compensation.
- **Adaptation from endorsement.** That someone learns to function within a defective system does not show that they chose, approve of, or benefit from the arrangement.
- **Compliance from consent.** Doing what circumstances require to survive or protect dependents is not free agreement.
- **Resilience from legitimacy.** The ability to endure deprivation does not make the deprivation reasonable.
- **Care from sacrifice.** Caring for another person does not imply an obligation to absorb unlimited institutional costs.
- **Competence from obligation.** Being capable of fixing a systemic failure does not establish that fixing it should become your personal responsibility.
- **Efficiency from cost.** An organization looks cheap or lean only because its real operational costs have been pushed outside its balance sheet.
- **Failure from its point of manifestation.** The exhausted clinician, overwhelmed patient, or burned-out caseworker is the location where system failure becomes visible, not where it originates.
- **Need from obligation.** Something needing to be done does not answer who should bear the burden of doing it. Institutions routinely collapse "someone must adapt" into "therefore this particular person should adapt."

The deepest separation is between the formal organization and the total system that produces the outcome:

```
Total Operating Architecture = Formal Organization + Uncounted Compensatory Inputs
```

Once separated, the missing distributional question re-emerges: who bears the cost of making the arrangement work, and why them?

## Taxonomy of compensatory inputs

While companion essays treat the macro-metabolic limits of [adaptive capacity](/research/theory/the-consumption-of-adaptive-capacity) and the metric bias of [absorption](/research/theory/absorption-as-concealment), auditing an institution requires categorizing the specific labor inputs that make up this shadow subsidy:

1. **Repair labor:** Fixing defects left behind by the formal process. Correcting corrupt records, working around defective software, and manually reconciling mismatched data schemas.
2. **Coordination labor:** Connecting fragmented parts of an organization that refuse to communicate. Passing records between departmental silos and tracking dependencies across disconnected teams.
3. **Interpretive labor:** Deciphering ambiguous instructions, translating institutional jargon, and reverse-engineering undocumented operating rules.
4. **Anticipatory labor:** Maintaining vigilance to predict catastrophic failures before they trip. Watching a fragile queue to catch dropped requests before they harm a client.
5. **Affective labor:** Absorbing institutional friction and frustration. Soothing angry users, de-escalating conflicts caused by procedural delays, and maintaining morale under chronic dysfunction.
6. **Self-regulatory labor:** Suppressing one's own physical, psychological, or schedule needs so the arrangement remains workable. Skipping meals or breaks, tolerating sensory overload, and masking distress to meet inflexible protocols.
7. **Risk-bearing labor:** Personally carrying uncertainty or legal liability that the institution has externalized downward without indemnity or safety nets.
8. **Temporal labor:** Waiting on hold, chasing missing paperwork, remembering unnotified deadlines, and rearranging personal life around institutional delays.

These inputs are usually folded into the background and misrecognized as personality, professionalism, dedication, or "just how things work."

## Distributed coercion: why adaptation is not voluntary

Organizations often defend these demands by treating participation as voluntary. If workers do not resign, or if claimants complete the paperwork, the institution assumes consent.

This is a category error. Coercion in modern institutions is rarely delivered through direct command. It is distributed across the surrounding arrangement:

- **Structural coercion:** Refusal guarantees that something critical fails. The nurse can refuse to reconcile records off the clock, but the consequence is patient harm.
- **Dependency coercion:** The vulnerability of people who depend on you makes withdrawal relationally and morally prohibitive.
- **Normative coercion:** Professionalism, dedication, maternal love, or citizenship turn refusal into a personal moral defect. The person who insists on the boundary is labeled uncooperative or uncaring.
- **Procedural coercion:** Formally optional behavior becomes necessary to navigate a defective system. The workaround is the only path to completing the assigned task.
- **Economic coercion:** The party with the least ability to exit ends up supplying the necessary adjustment.

When the alternative to adaptation is catastrophic for oneself or others, adaptation is functionally compelled.

## Turning adaptation into character

To legitimize this extraction, institutions reclassify structural coping strategies as personal virtues:

- A person repeatedly saves a broken process, and the institution calls them **dependable**.
- A person tolerates chronic instability, and the institution calls them **flexible**.
- A person absorbs interpersonal hostility, and the institution calls them **professional**.
- A person anticipates recurring failures, and the institution calls them **experienced**.
- A person endures prolonged deprivation, and the institution calls them **resilient**.

This vocabulary attaches the accomplishment to the worker's character while leaving the originating defect untouched. It converts institutional debt into supposed human capital.

The extraordinarily capable worker who prevents mistakes is not merely an asset. Repeated dependence on their vigilance represents an unrecorded liability. When an organization praises a team for being "easy to manage," it usually means the team privately supplies enormous quantities of accommodation to the organization.

The presumed direction of accommodation flips:

```
Conventional assumption: Institution → accommodates → Person
Operational reality:     Person      → accommodates → Institution
```

## The analytical reversal

Ethotechnical evaluation reverses the conventional managerial inquiry:

> Instead of asking why this person failed to adapt, ask what their adaptation had previously been preventing the institution from having to confront.

When a worker collapses, a student drops out, or a patient gives up, conventional metrics treat that individual as the point of failure. Under this framework, they are merely the point of manifestation: the location where the cost of institutional incompleteness could no longer be privatized.

## What the laws take from this

The framework takes five constraints from this analysis:

1. **Correction obligation (Law IV):** Every consequential delegation creates an obligation to correct its errors. If an institution relies on uncounted human repair to stay viable, that capacity must be accounted for as an operational debit.
2. **Proportional standing (Law VII):** Error-bearing parties who supply the compensatory subsidy require standing to force upstream rule revision. Without standing, adaptation is uncompensated extraction.
3. **Non-theatrical observability (Law VIII):** Measuring burden by asking exhausted people to fill out surveys repeats the extraction. Burden must be read from the system's own trail of retries, abandonments, and off-hours activity.
4. **Highest-layer evaluation (Law X):** Evaluation must sit at the highest layer where harm emerges: in the lives and households of the people who absorb the mismatch, not within the boundaries of the model or software code.
5. **Preserving contestability (Law XII):** No system may consume the finite adaptive capacity required to contest it. An institution that exhausts its participants until they can no longer object wins by attrition, not legitimacy.
