A model evaluation establishes properties of a model. It cannot establish the alignment of the system that model is deployed into, because that system includes the people who notice what the model got wrong, interpret what it left ambiguous, and absorb what it broke. Where those corrections are routine rather than exceptional, the people making them are not users of the safety architecture. They are part of it.

This essay is about what follows from that, and why it follows with more force than a general appeal to participation would.

## The asymmetry

Consider an institution that deploys an automated system into a population and then relies on that population to catch what the system misses. A caseworker who quietly re-enters a rejected form. A nurse who double-checks a dose the scheduler set. A customer who calls three times until someone reverses the charge. Each of these people is performing a safety function. The institution's error rate, as reported, is the rate after they have done their work.

Now ask what authority each of them holds. Usually none. They cannot stop the system, cannot change its parameters, cannot make it reconsider a class of decisions, and often cannot make it acknowledge that the correction happened. The institution has delegated a safety function without delegating the power to exercise it. It asks for judgment and refuses the standing that judgment implies.

That is the asymmetry. It is not a matter of degree. An institution can depend on a population's labor and pay for it; that is an ordinary economic relation. An institution that depends on a population's corrective capacity and gives it no route into the system's governing state has put the population in a position where their competence is required and their voice is not. The political theory term for a relation of that shape is domination: one party's welfare depends on the other's forbearance, and the dependent party has no recognized means of contesting how the forbearance is exercised.

## Why the narrow claim holds where the broad one does not

There is a familiar argument that everyone affected by a system should have a say in it. It is a fine argument and it is not the one being made here. The broad argument runs into an obvious objection: affectedness is everywhere, and a system that had to consult everyone it touched would never act. Institutions rightly reject that.

The narrow claim survives the objection because it is about a specific kind of labor. Ordinary productive labor makes a system valuable. Extracting it without a say is a distributive wrong, and distributive wrongs can, in principle, be paid off. Corrective labor does something further. Absorbing a failure competently removes the evidence that it occurred. The metric improves because someone caught it. The institution therefore loses not only the labor but its own capacity to know how the system is performing.

This is the fail-silent state in which the absorbing humans are the mechanism. Nothing in the institution's instruments registers the failure, because the failure was handled before the instrument looked. Dependence on correction is self-concealing in a way that dependence on production is not. A factory that runs on unpaid labor at least knows it has a workforce. An institution that runs on unacknowledged correction may sincerely believe its system is healthy, and its dashboards will agree.

So the narrow claim is not that affected people deserve a say because they are affected. It is that people whose corrections the institution depends on hold information the institution cannot otherwise get, and that withholding standing from them is not only unjust but epistemically self-defeating. A clean dashboard cannot be read as evidence of health until absorption has been measured separately.

## What standing has to mean

If the argument is right, the remedy has to be more than consultation. Being asked for feedback does not change the relation; the institution can still ignore what it hears, and the corrective labor continues regardless. What changes the relation is standing: a recognized route by which the person bearing the error can put that error into the system's governing state, where it has to be answered and can produce a change.

Standing in this sense is procedural. It does not mean a veto over the system. It means that a challenge, once raised by someone with standing, must be received by an identified responder, answered by a stated deadline against a stated standard, and be capable of producing one of a defined set of outcomes: the decision stands, is narrowed, is suspended, is reversed, or the authority that produced it is reconsidered. That is what turns an observation into a control. Without it, the observation is a complaint.

This also says what standing should not track. Standing that attaches to current indispensability rewards staying indispensable, which is the opposite of what a safety architecture wants. Standing should attach to dependence that has been documented, and it should survive the dependence being engineered away. Otherwise the doctrine preserves the fragility it exists to expose.

## The bound on the claim

The argument grounds a demand, and it also bounds it. Authority follows demonstrated dependence, so it is proportional rather than unlimited. The institution is not obliged to give everyone who touches the system a voice in its design. It is obliged to give the people whose corrections it relies on a route into its state, with force proportional to how far it relies on them.

That proportionality needs an instrument. The question is not whether the system has users who sometimes complain. It is how far the system's reported performance depends on absorption that is not being counted. Where that share is large, the population is doing the institution's safety work, and the case for standing is strong. Where it is small, the case is weaker and the institution can say so with evidence rather than assertion.

## What the laws take from this

The method does not require anyone to accept the argument above. It takes from it three things and states them as constraints.

The first is that every consequential delegation creates a correction obligation, because the institution that delegates authority to act has also delegated the capacity to be wrong, and someone will bear that. The second is that error-bearing parties require standing proportional to exposure, in the procedural sense just described. The third is that observability without state transition is theater, because the whole point of standing is that what the error-bearing party sees can change what the system does.

A reader who rejects the theory of domination can still adopt those constraints on engineering grounds: a system whose failures are absorbed silently is a system whose failure rate its operators do not know. The theory explains why the constraints are owed. The method only insists that they be met.
