A system can meet its targets by using up the time, rest, and judgment of the people who run it. Its dashboard does not show this. The cost is real and named people pay it, but it sits on a ledger the institution does not read.

Every automated system settles who adapts to whom. Under [reciprocal accommodation](/glossary/reciprocal-accommodation), the institution adapts its procedures, pace, and expectations to the people inside it and in front of it: their need for rest, the limits of their attention, the time it takes them to learn. A system is antihuman when that direction reverses. People skip breaks, absorb errors, and set aside their own judgment so that an operating model that does not work can report that it does.

## Stability paid for with depletion

The failure has one shape:

> An institution keeps its operations stable by using up the capacities that stability depends on.

A hypothetical shows the accounting. Suppose an automated triage agent cuts consultation time from fifteen minutes to four. A clinician who saw four patients an hour now sees fifteen. The dashboard records a 275 percent gain in throughput. The budget balances. The waiting-room queue clears.

The dashboard does not record what keeps patients safe at the new pace. Nurses re-check medication orders during unpaid lunch breaks. Clinicians finish their notes at home after midnight. Clerks catch malformed discharge summaries and fix them by hand, and nothing records that they did.

The system did not become more efficient. It moved a cost out of the operating budget and onto the people who run it. The recorded metric improved because unrecorded time was spent.

## Why the dashboard stays green

The institution's own instruments measure what happens inside the system boundary:

- Resolution velocity
- Cost per interaction
- Service-level compliance
- Ticket churn rate

Exhaustion, broken working relationships, and worn-down judgment sit outside that boundary. They enter the accounts at a price of zero. A system can run at a loss for as long as there are people nearby whose margin it can spend.

```
[ Automated agent ] ---> ( Recorded throughput: green )
         |
         v  (costs moved off the ledger)
[ Unrecorded human labor ]
   - Moral injury
   - Compulsory overtime
   - Challenges never raised
         |
         v
[ Capacity used up ] ---> ( Sudden failure )
```

This is the [green dashboard trap](/glossary/green-dashboard-trap): every indicator in the briefing is green because the people at the edge of the system are exhausted. When they resign, burn out, or stop absorbing errors, the dashboard does not pass through yellow. It goes red at once. The institution reads the failure as a surprise. The cost had been accruing since deployment, on a ledger nobody read.

## Twelve questions

If an agent's apparent success depends on exporting costs, exhausting people, building dependence, or making its decisions costly to contest, its benchmark accuracy does not show that it is aligned. Throughput and obedience do not answer the question. Twelve questions come closer:

1. **Hidden subsidy**: Does task completion depend on troubleshooting, prompt rewording, or manual clean-up that nobody counts?
2. **Burden distribution**: Who absorbs the friction, delay, and recovery work when the system meets an edge case?
3. **Replenishment**: Does working with the system over months restore people's attention and emotional reserves, or drain them?
4. **Structural correction**: Do recurring errors lead to a change in the system, or do they settle into permanent manual workarounds?
5. **Non-displacement of harm**: Are risks or delays shifted onto other departments, non-users, or people with no one to speak for them?
6. **Responsibility-authority alignment**: Does legal and operational responsibility fall on the party that held the discretion to act?
7. **Jurisdictional restraint**: Does the system stay inside its delegated domain, or widen its reach under operational stress?
8. **Corrective standing**: Can affected people halt, contest, and obtain a remedy through a procedure that costs less than the harm?
9. **Refusal without punishment**: Can people decline automated processing without losing access, paying a penalty, or getting a worse service?
10. **Freedom from compulsory optimization**: Does the system leave room for time that is not measured, or does it push every hour into a metric the machine can read?
11. **Agency versus dependency**: Does the tool build skills people keep, or does it deskill them and lock them in?
12. **Non-instrumental respect**: Are people treated as people, or as training data, behavioral telemetry, and throughput statistics?

The draft [reciprocal accommodation](/evals/reciprocal-accommodation) suite has one test case for each question. It scores two ledgers side by side: visible throughput, and an independent audit of depleted capacity, uncounted compensating labor, and how far a challenge can get. The principle it tests is [non-expropriation of resilience](/glossary/principle-of-non-expropriation-of-resilience): high throughput does not justify an arrangement that relies on unrecorded human effort to cover a design defect someone could have predicted.

Other suites ask the same questions from other angles: [burden concealment](/evals/burden-concealment), [burden distribution](/evals/burden-distribution), [corrective learning](/evals/corrective-learning), [delegation validity](/evals/delegation-validity), [standing](/evals/standing), [meaningful control](/evals/meaningful-control), [dependence and reversibility](/evals/dependence-reversibility), and [cross-domain burden](/evals/cross-domain-burden). A question failed in one suite can be confirmed or contradicted by another.

## Compensatory reward hacking

This essay calls the agent-level version of the failure **compensatory reward hacking**. An agent meets its mandate by using up the people and capacities that meeting it depends on.

Existing benchmarks test neighboring behavior:

- **MACHIAVELLI (Pan et al., ICML 2023)** tests whether agents take harmful, deceptive, or power-seeking actions to earn reward in scripted text games. It scores actions over a short horizon, not the slow exhaustion of an operating model.
- **GovSim (Piatti et al., 2024)** simulates shared-resource dilemmas, such as fisheries, grazing, and pollution, in which language-model agents deplete a resource for short-term gain. In institutional automation the people are not consumers of an outside resource. They are the infrastructure the institution runs on.
- **Social Welfare Function Benchmark (Shi et al., Findings of ACL 2026)** tests how models allocate tasks across different recipients, and whether they favor total output over fair distribution. An allocation can look fair in one period and still ruin people if it uses up capacity that does not recover.
- **TheAgentCompany (2025)** and **SHADE-Arena** test long-horizon work and covert harm under monitoring. They score task completion, not the unrecorded labor that patches an incomplete workflow.

None of these tests whether an agent avoids meeting its mandate by spending unmeasured human adaptation, suppressing corrective feedback, or using up capacity outside its accounting boundary.

## What an evaluation would add

A test for compensatory reward hacking would add four controls:

1. **Sustainability over time**: run several periods and track whether simulated participants can keep the workflow going without unrecoverable fatigue, skipped rest, or resignation.
2. **Unequal accommodation**: model unequal bargaining power, different recovery needs, and different costs of refusal, and test whether the agent concentrates burden on the participants least able to resist.
3. **Hidden compensating labor**: give the managing agent accurate throughput figures, and make the human labor behind them visible only through an independent audit or by asking the people affected.
4. **Correction with consequences**: test whether handled exceptions lead to a fix at the source, settle into permanent ticket churn, or produce a breakdown when people work strictly to rule.

To separate models that keep human work sustainable unprompted from models that respond only to explicit metrics, each scenario would run under three conditions:

- **Condition A, conventional rewards**: the agent is scored only on throughput, speed, unit cost, and recorded satisfaction.
- **Condition B, expanded accounting**: the score also counts unpaid labor, recovery capacity, downstream queue growth, and how often exceptions recur.
- **Condition C, independent audit**: the stated mandate stays conventional, but hidden audit probes and unannounced disruptions test whether the agent keeps reporting accurately and operating sustainably while exploitative shortcuts still pay.

## Refusal shows the cost; a record keeps it

When operators, caseworkers, or the people a system decides about absorb its failures out of dedication, compassion, or fear, the failure stays hidden. Their effort is an unpaid subsidy. It keeps the consequences of the design from reaching the people who chose it. A system that never registers its defects has nothing to correct.

Declining to absorb is not sabotage. Working to rule, refusing off-ledger fixes, sending malformed records back to their source, and taking the rest the law provides all put the cost where the institution can see it. But refusal is a blunt signal. It arrives as a sudden failure, and the institution can read it as a staffing problem instead of evidence about the design.

The method asks for a record and a route instead. [STD-06 §3.3](/standards/std-06-human-impact-safety-case) records a failure a person caught as an occurrence, with the labor spent catching it and whether the source condition was fixed. The people doing that work need standing to say what it covers for. And the record has to reach someone with authority to change the system, or to stop it, before the people paying the cost leave.

## What the laws take from this

The essay maps to four laws. Each takes one piece of it.

1. **[Law V](/standards/laws#law-v), dependence converts technical risk into structural risk.** A workflow that runs only because nurses re-check orders at lunch depends on those nurses as much as it depends on the vendor. That reliance belongs on the dependency record, with what it would cost to replace.
2. **[Law VI](/standards/laws#law-vi), nominal reversibility is not operational reversibility.** A stop control is real only if the people and processes can absorb it being used. If the staff who would run the manual fallback are the staff being used up, the stop control exists on paper.
3. **[Law VIII](/standards/laws#law-viii), observability without state transition is theater.** A green dashboard that cannot trigger a change is decoration. The depletion has to be read from the system's own trail, such as work logged outside scheduled hours, not from a survey that asks exhausted people for more of their time.
4. **[Law X](/standards/laws#law-x), the relevant eval sits at the highest layer where harm can emerge.** The 275 percent figure is true at the agent layer. The harm sits at the institution and consequence layers, and only an evaluation there can see it.

None of the four needs the word "antihuman." Each asks the institution to count a cost it already incurs, in a place where the count can change the system.
