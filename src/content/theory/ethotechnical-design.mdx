A person's capacity to adapt is finite, and who spends it is a political question. Ethotechnical design asks not only whether an arrangement works. It asks what adjustment the arrangement demands from people, who bears that demand, and whether they can force the arrangement to change.

The essay on [what Ethotechnics is not](/research/theory/what-ethotechnics-is-not) draws the discipline's boundary. The essay on [what outcomes hide](/research/theory/what-outcomes-hide) applies it to evaluation. This one states it as a design practice.

## Who adapts

Every design is an imperfect model of reality. A form, a workflow, a building, an algorithm, a rule, an interface, or an institution sets expectations about the people and situations it will meet. Some reality will not fit.

The ordinary design question is: _how do we make people interact successfully with this system?_ The ethotechnical question inverts it: _when the system and the person do not fit, why should the person be the thing that changes?_

So the discipline is about who is made to adapt:

> design → mismatch → a need to adapt → someone assigned to meet it

A badly designed arrangement keeps assigning that need downward, to the people with the least say. Users remember passwords. Patients coordinate providers. Workers repair software. Disabled people request exceptions. Citizens reproduce information government already possesses. Caregivers integrate fragmented services. The arrangement stays stable because people bend around it.

Ethotechnical design reverses the direction:

> **Spend institutional capacity before consuming [human adaptive capacity](/glossary/adaptive-capacity).**

That does not mean people should never adapt. Learning, cooperation, compromise, care, and collective life all require it. The distinction is between adaptation that is productive or mutual and [**compensatory adaptation**](/glossary/compensatory-adaptation): effort a person spends mainly because an arrangement failed to accommodate something it could have foreseen.

## Four moves

The designer works through four moves.

**First, eliminate the demand where possible.** Before improving a form, ask whether the form needs to exist. Before making compliance easier, ask whether compliance is necessary. Before designing an accommodation process, ask whether the default could accommodate more variation.

**Second, absorb complexity upstream.** Where complexity is necessary, place it where there is the greatest capacity to handle it. Institutions can integrate databases, coordinate departments, hire expertise, maintain infrastructure, automate repetitive tasks, and learn across cases. Isolated individuals usually cannot.

**Third, design for the remainder.** No arrangement will anticipate everyone. An exception is not necessarily evidence of bad design. Some exceptions are certain. Ethotechnical design therefore includes low-cost exception handling, human judgment, escalation, reversibility, appeal, and a way to keep operating when the model meets something it did not anticipate.

**Fourth, let repeated mismatch redesign the system.** This separates accommodation from correction. If thousands of people need the same exception, the system should not get better at processing thousands of exceptions. It should ask whether the rule producing them is wrong.

## Unfinished design

The four moves assume that design is deliberately unfinished. No amount of anticipation eliminates mismatch. A conventional designer can treat the specification as authoritative and the exception as deviation. The ethotechnical designer treats the specification as a hypothesis and the exception as possible evidence.

That changes how the designer reads failure. When users keep working around something, the workaround is evidence about the design, not only clever behavior. When frontline workers keep exercising unofficial discretion, the formal process may be inadequate. When disabled people keep requesting the same accommodation, the question is not how fast the requests can be processed. It is what the requests reveal about the default.

> **The remainder is information about the design.**

## Following the friction

The discipline is suspicious of frictionlessness, because friction rarely disappears. It moves. A one-click service may depend on warehouse workers absorbing more volatile schedules. An automated bureaucracy may save staff time while making citizens troubleshoot their own classifications. A healthcare portal with no visible friction may make patients responsible for assembling information that administrative staff used to handle.

Instead of asking whether a design is frictionless, ethotechnical analysis follows the friction: _who is doing the work now?_ The same applies to efficiency. An organization whose internal costs fall has not necessarily become more efficient. It may have moved the costs outside. So evaluation covers everyone who has to adapt, not only the people inside the organization. The [falsified denominator](/glossary/falsified-denominator) names the accounting error this avoids: an efficiency figure that counts the organization's time and treats everyone else's as free.

This widens accessibility. Accessibility is not a feature added for a subset of users. Human variation, whether bodily, cognitive, temporal, linguistic, or circumstantial, is an ordinary fact a system should expect. When some people cannot compensate for a design, they may expose a burden that better-resourced people have been absorbing without comment. The [workaround presumption](/glossary/workaround-presumption), the rule that a recurring workaround is first read as evidence of an upstream design failure, applies the same reading to coping.

## What it is not

Its object is who bears the burden of adapting and whether they can get it corrected. That is why it cannot be reduced to universal design, human-centered design, participatory design, service design, ergonomics, or accessibility, although it borrows from each. Its view of participation follows: it values participation but does not assume more is better. Making people constantly report problems, attend meetings, submit feedback, monitor decisions, and organize for basic corrections creates another adaptation burden.

The objective is **high corrective power at low human cost**. People should not have to become experts in the institution that governs them to make it behave reasonably. The test for any arrangement is two questions. First: _what does this design require people to notice, remember, learn, endure, coordinate, translate, correct, prove, monitor, or repeatedly request — and which of those tasks could the arrangement itself absorb?_ Second, the political question: _when those requirements become unreasonable, can the people bearing them make the design change?_

Those two questions separate the discipline from making something pleasant or convenient. Its aim is not maximum ease, personalization, or efficiency. It is that the institution spends its own staff, budget, and systems on complexity so that people do not spend their evenings on it.

## What the laws take from this

Each of the four moves needs something an institution can be held to. Four laws supply it.

[Law IV](/standards/laws#law-iv): every consequential delegation creates a correction obligation. That is the fourth move. Repeated mismatch has to be able to change the rule that produces it, not only be absorbed more efficiently.

[Law VII](/standards/laws#law-vii): error-bearing parties require standing proportional to exposure. That is the second test question, whether the people bearing an unreasonable demand can make the design change.

[Law VIII](/standards/laws#law-viii): observability without state transition is theater. It separates a system that logs exceptions from one that is changed by them.

[Law XI](/standards/laws#law-xi): successful automation increases its own governance burden. Every internal step a system saves is a demand that has landed somewhere — when it has landed. Some steps disappear without landing anywhere: a duplicate entry, an obsolete approval, a preventable error. The law does not assume the transfer; it asks of each saving whether a demand appeared elsewhere, and makes the next expansion a decision that has to say where.

A designer who rejects the politics can still use the test questions. A system that spends its users' capacity instead of its own has not become cheaper to run. It has moved the bill to people who do not appear in its accounts.
