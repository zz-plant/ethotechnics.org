Institutions are in the business of demanding that human beings adapt. A patient must learn the patient portal. A clinician must master the alert routing queue. A welfare claimant must compile three years of bank statements. A worker must absorb the erratic schedule a scheduling algorithm produces.

Most governance frameworks ask whether these demands are legally authorized, procedurally uniform, or technically efficient. Almost none ask the foundational question:

> Under what conditions, if any, may an institution legitimately consume another person's finite capacity to cope?

When an arrangement answers that question by silence, it treats people's capacity to cope as unlimited and free. That silence produces the [human crumple zone](/glossary/crumple-zone): the person who absorbs the cost and the blame when a system fails, the way a car's crumple zone absorbs a crash.

## A limited resource

Human beings do not possess infinite resilience. The capacities required to get through an institution's friction — sustained attention, working memory, emotional regulation, patience, off-hours paperwork, vigilance against automated errors, and care for others — are limited by the body and by the clock. This essay calls them adaptive capacity. Every hour an individual spends deciphering a contradictory bureaucratic notice or manually translating data between two incompatible software systems is an hour subtracted from sleep, caregiving, creative craft, and ordinary life.

Feminist political economists call the background work that keeps people functioning from one day to the next [social reproduction](/research/theory/what-ethotechnics-is-not). Adaptive capacity is what that work runs on.

When an institution designs a system that leaks errors, it does not eliminate those errors; it routes them downward. Frontline clinicians stay late to reconcile missing lab reports. Desperate parents spend days on hold attempting to correct automated benefit terminations. Because this work happens outside anything the institution measures, the institution's dashboard reports smooth operation and lean overhead. But the cost has not disappeared: it has been externalized into the bodies and lives of the people least equipped to refuse it.

## The cost nobody books

An externality is a cost one party imposes on others without paying for it. The cost this essay is about has a name: the [adaptation externality](/glossary/adaptation-externality), the coping an institution's defects demand from the people around it.

It hides in the institution's own accounts. What the institution reports as its performance has two parts, a split the method calls [performance decomposition](/glossary/performance-decomposition):

```
observed performance = designed capacity + human compensation
```

An institution that weighs observed performance against its own operating costs counts what people's coping produces and leaves out what it costs them. Its throughput looks high because people it does not pay are paying for part of it.

People are kept in the loop to catch the system's errors and take the blame for them, not to use their judgment. The glossary calls this [counterfeit buffering](/glossary/counterfeit-buffering). Their effort keeps the system from failing visibly, and that produces the evidence that the design is sound. By absorbing the impact, they keep the institution from seeing that it needs to change.

## Genuine versus manufactured necessity

Not all friction is illegitimate. Living in a material world with mortal bodies, limited energy, and complex coordination requires real effort. The crucial task is distinguishing **genuine necessity** from **manufactured necessity**.

| Dimension                  | Genuine necessity                                                                                 | Manufactured necessity                                                                                          |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Origin**                 | Irreducible physical scarcity, mortality, natural complexity, open-ended collaboration.           | Unreconciled databases, rigid software schemas, departmental silos, budget caps on intake staff.                |
| **Alternative**            | No feasible institutional redesign can eliminate the friction without violating physical reality. | Friction could be eliminated by reallocating the institution's internal budget or automating internal handoffs. |
| **Burden distribution**    | Shared collectively or absorbed through reciprocal, recognized care.                              | Asymmetric: concentrated downward on the claimant, patient, or frontline worker.                                |
| **Institutional response** | Transparent acknowledgment; continuous effort to buffer the vulnerable.                           | Defensive denial; treating the friction as "fraud prevention" or the subject's personal incompetence.           |

**Genuine necessity** is the friction of the world. It is the complex clinical reality of a patient with five interacting chronic illnesses; it is the physical difficulty of repairing a power grid after a storm. Meeting it calls for skill and judgment, freely given.

**Manufactured necessity** is the friction of uncorrected institutional architecture. It is the requirement that an applicant re-verify their identity four times across three agencies that refuse to share records. It is the nurse having to click through seventy irrelevant pop-up alerts before administering medication. It persists because, for the institution, transferring the burden of coping onto participants is cheaper than fixing the upstream rule.

When an institution justifies manufactured necessity by calling it unavoidable, it commits a category error: it mistakes the limits of its own budget for the laws of nature.

## What counts as progress

Treating adaptive capacity as a limited resource changes what counts as institutional progress.

Reform usually focuses on **making people better at coping**: teaching claimants how to navigate convoluted portals, training clinicians in "resilience" and mindfulness, or deploying consumer-facing AI agents to automate the filing of appeals.

While coping tools may provide temporary relief, treating them as progress is perverse. When institutions discover that people have become better at coping, they routinely raise the baseline demand. Better consumer tools are met with more complex administrative obstacles, raising the minimum stamina required to secure basic rights.

The test Ethotechnics applies is the opposite:

> Progress is not making people better at enduring bad systems. It is making the system **depend less on people with exceptional stamina or skill**.

An arrangement is not just because a hero can navigate it. It is just only when an exhausted, distracted, non-expert person with ordinary human limitations can live inside it with dignity, without having their life consumed by the process of survival.

## Architectural constraints: the binding laws

The principle connects to three of the laws:

### 1. The correction obligation (Law IV)

Every delegated automated action creates an institutional obligation to correct its errors. If an automated decision generates a false rejection, the burden of discovering, documenting, and contesting that mistake cannot be externalized onto the affected subject. The time and adaptive capacity consumed by the correction process must be tracked as an operational debit against the system's authorization grant.

### 2. Proportional standing for error-bearing parties (Law VII)

Whoever absorbs the adaptation externality must hold [corrective standing](/glossary/corrective-standing) capable of forcing upstream rule revision. If an institution relies on frontline nurses or benefit recipients to patch its defects, those actors cannot be confined to advisory committees or customer feedback surveys. They must possess the formal standing to introduce counterevidence that invalidates the operating model's assumptions.

### 3. Preserving the conditions of contestability (Law XII)

No system may consume the very resources required to contest it. An appeal process that requires thirty hours of legal research and paperwork from an unhoused person violates Law XII: it wins by attrition, using exhaustion to manufacture acquiescence. A legitimate system must ensure that the friction of contestation is lower than the friction of submission.

## Conclusion: the right to unconsumed life

To ask what an institution may demand is ultimately to ask what human life is for.

Human beings were not born to serve as shock absorbers for inflexible databases. They were not created to spend their finite days translating between bureaucratic categories that cannot perceive them.

The goal of [ethotechnical design](/research/theory/ethotechnical-design) is not a friction-free society, nor is it a fully automated world where machines replace human relation. It is the careful construction of institutions that respect the physical limits of the people living inside them: systems that absorb their own errors, account for their own burdens, and return to ordinary people the one thing no balance sheet can restore: their own time.
