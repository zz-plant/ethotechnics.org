A constitution may be amendable and practically impossible to amend. A
cooperative may hold democratic votes and depend on a lender it cannot
challenge. A public may have the right to organize and lack the time, money,
information, or institutional leverage to make its demands consequential.

Each arrangement satisfies a formal test of revisability. None gives the people
subject to it effective corrective power. The difference is not a detail of
implementation. It is the difference between a right and a remedy.

This essay states what has to be true for correction to be effective, how a
claim travels when the cause sits above the deciding body, and what an
institution's ordinary operation does to the capacity for correction over time.
It extends the account of [case and structural
corrigibility](/research/theory/exception-learning) and the
[dependence](/research/theory/dependence-without-standing) and
[capture](/research/theory/automation-and-capture) arguments already in the
corpus.

## The corrective power chain

Contestability is often treated as a binary: a challenge route exists, or it
does not. That test is too weak. Correction runs through five conditions, and a
break at any one leaves the later conditions decorative.

| Condition | The question | A failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Detection** | Can people establish what the arrangement is doing? | A black-box model, an unauditable record, or a cost that never appears in any account |
| **Standing** | Can affected people challenge it without permission from those responsible? | A grievance route that runs through the person being grieved |
| **Agenda control** | Can they require the underlying problem to be considered? | A process that can correct the case but cannot ask whether the rule is wrong |
| **Decision power** | Can their intervention alter the rule, authority, or resource allocation responsible? | An advisory committee, a consultation, or a review with no binding effect |
| **Enforcement** | Can the resulting decision be implemented despite resistance? | A remedy that requires the losing party to execute it, with no consequence for refusal |

The chain is a test, not a score. An institution can be strong at detection and
weak at enforcement, or grant standing and deny agenda control. The point is to
name the link that fails before calling the arrangement corrigible.

[Law VI](/standards/laws#law-vi) already separates technical, operational, and
institutional reversibility. The chain adds the political conditions that sit
between them. A halt control that works is not a correction if the people who
need it cannot reach it, if the challenge cannot name the rule, or if the
decision can be ignored.

The operational form is [MEC-26 Effective corrective
power](/mechanisms/patterns/effective-corrective-power).

## Jurisdictional escalation

Many failures are experienced at one level and caused at another. A worker
contests a scheduling decision. Management explains that staffing limits
prevent change. The staffing limits follow from a budget. The budget follows
from a financing arrangement. The financing arrangement reflects an investment
decision outside the enterprise.

The worker's complaint may be entirely justified, and no decision-maker inside
the organization may hold enough authority to address its cause. A framework
that stops at the employing institution turns structural constraint into a
personal failure to persuade the right manager.

Jurisdictional escalation supplies the missing rule: when an institution cannot
remedy a verified burden because the binding constraint sits above it, the
claim must be able to travel to the body that sets the constraint. The claim
does not automatically win. It gains standing in the forum that can actually
change the condition.

The rule has a reciprocal limit. Higher-level institutions should not
automatically appropriate decisions that people can govern effectively at a
lower level. Escalation is for constraints that cannot be reached locally, not
a warrant for centralizing everything. [MEC-26 Effective corrective
power](/mechanisms/patterns/effective-corrective-power) carries both the chain
and the escalation rule.

## Error is not disagreement

The principle that people an arrangement gets wrong should be able to revise it
is persuasive when there is an identifiable mistake. Many political conflicts
are not mistakes. A housing policy can benefit tenants and impose real costs on
small property owners. An environmental restriction can protect future
residents and eliminate present livelihoods. Both sides can have genuine
standing, and their claims cannot always be reconciled.

If every burdened group could reverse the decision that burdened it, collective
decisions would be impossible. Corrigibility cannot mean a universal veto.

The distinction is between an **error** and a **settled disagreement**. An error
is a failure against the institution's own rules, evidence, or stated purpose;
it calls for a remedy. A disagreement is a conflict between legitimate claims;
it calls for a legitimate procedure, a reasoned decision, and a settlement that
does not destroy the losing side's capacity to challenge the next decision.

That last condition is the one institutions most often fail. The minority does
not need to win. It needs to retain the resources, organization, information,
and standing to survive the loss and contest what comes next. A settlement that
impoverishes, disperses, or disorganizes the losers has not resolved a
disagreement; it has removed one side from future politics.

This is why [MEC-27 Protected
disagreement](/mechanisms/patterns/protected-disagreement) is a pattern about
what the losing side keeps, not about who wins. It also marks the limit of this
framework. Corrigibility is a condition of legitimate collective
decision-making. It is not a theory of justice, and it does not decide how
scarce resources should be distributed.

## Non-self-extinguishing authority

A constitutional order can be amended into an order that abolishes independent
elections, restricts opposition, and makes further amendment practically
impossible. The original decision may be procedurally authorized. Its result
destroys the conditions of future correction.

If everything is revisable, can people legitimately revise away the capacity
for revision? The framework has reason to say no. That answer does not freeze
existing constitutions, property relations, or governing institutions. It
distinguishes changing an arrangement from extinguishing the continuing
capacity of the people subject to it to participate in changing it.

[Law XII](/standards/laws#law-xii) states the narrower version: no system may
erase the conditions of its own contestability. The wider version is a
commitment, not a derivation. It says that no exercise of constituent or
delegated authority may permanently eliminate the effective means through which
those subject to it can contest and revise its future exercise.

The commitment is normative, and it should be stated as one. It is also
incomplete without a theory of which changes count as extinguishing capacity.
A change that narrows a particular route may leave other routes intact; a change
that looks procedural may remove the material conditions that made the route
real. The test is not whether the old form survives. It is whether the people
subject to the new form retain effective means of future correction.

## Independent corrective capacity

An institution can become harder to challenge without formally restricting
contestation. It can develop skills useful mainly inside its own arrangement,
make livelihoods depend on its continued operation, define which grievances are
recognizable, reward adaptation to its categories of success, and make
alternatives difficult to imagine, organize, or finance.

The result can be fewer complaints, higher satisfaction, better measured
performance, and less capacity for transformative change. A conventional
corrigibility assessment can mistake that for success. The absence of
opposition is not evidence of legitimacy.

The test is therefore not only whether people successfully challenge existing
arrangements. It is whether they retain the developed capabilities, independent
resources, collective organizations, and practical alternatives needed to
challenge them. This is the dynamic version of [the consumption of adaptive
capacity](/research/theory/the-consumption-of-adaptive-capacity): an
institution's ordinary operation can consume the conditions of its own future
correction.

[MEC-28 Reproductive
conditions](/mechanisms/patterns/reproductive-conditions) treats those
conditions as an object of governance. The measure is difficult, and an
independent evaluator can impose a contested judgment about what people ought
to want. The pattern therefore stays with observable conditions of agency —
skills, resources, organizations, alternatives, and the time to use them —
rather than claiming access to anyone's authentic preferences.

## The correction window

Correction is usually imagined as a feedback loop: an arrangement acts, an
error is discovered, and the arrangement changes. Institutional consequences do
not necessarily remain reversible while correction is organized. An ecosystem
can collapse. A community can be displaced. A capability can disappear. A
technical standard can create dependencies that make replacement prohibitively
expensive.

An arrangement can be fully amendable in principle and effectively irreversible
in practice. The relevant question is temporal: can affected people recognize
the harm, organize a challenge, and secure a response before the consequences
become irreversible or the cost of correction becomes prohibitive?

The answer is not a universal veto against irreversible choices. It is a
distinction among the gravity of the potential loss, the availability of
alternatives, and the distribution of the risk. Where the loss is severe and
the alternative is available, the burden of justification shifts toward
precaution, staged implementation, reversible experimentation, and protection
for people who cannot yet participate, including future generations.

## Technical infrastructure is constitutional

Software architectures, protocols, identity systems, data models, and platforms
determine who can do what, which actions are observable, and what kinds of
change remain feasible. A political community can democratically control an
organization and depend on proprietary systems that make meaningful revision
impractical.

A municipality may hold the legal authority to redesign a benefits system and
lack the ability to inspect, modify, migrate, or replace the software through
which benefits are administered. Formally, the rule is revisable.
Operationally, its implementation is not.

Where technical infrastructure materially governs people's lives, correction
requires more than a legal route. It requires the ability to inspect the
system, challenge its outputs, modify or replace it, and leave it without
unacceptable loss. Interoperability, data portability, independent auditing,
technical literacy, and credible alternatives are corrective infrastructure,
not procurement preferences.

Technical capacity is not democratic authority, and democratic authority does
not supply technical capacity. The pattern has to hold both.

## Accountable transition

When an institution must be replaced, the people who depend on it do not
disappear with it. An institution can reproduce domination and also support
livelihoods, services, skills, and communities. Removing it can be justified
without making every consequence of removal just.

The transition is part of the correction. It should be evaluated by how it
allocates losses, preserves essential capacities, sustains alternative
arrangements, and gives affected people authority over implementation. This is
not an argument for preserving harmful institutions indefinitely. It is an
argument against treating removal as sufficient evidence of emancipation.

The distinction the pattern has to keep is between justified accommodation and
an incumbent's attempt to preserve its position. A transition that protects
people is not the same as a transition that protects the institution from
change.

## The rules of recursion

A recursive process needs more than the instruction to revise. It needs rules
for starting, moving between levels, and treating a result as settled. Without
them, recursive corrigibility fails in four ways: endless procedural
contestation, entrenchment, self-abolition, or formal assurance with no
practical consequence.

| Requirement | Political question |
| --- | --- |
| **Entry** | Who can initiate a challenge, and on what grounds? |
| **Escalation** | When must a challenge move from a decision to a rule, an institution, or a wider structure? |
| **Authority** | Who may resolve the challenge, and what makes that authority legitimate? |
| **Closure** | When may a decision be provisionally settled despite continuing disagreement? |
| **Reopening** | Under what circumstances can the settlement be challenged again? |
| **Preservation** | What prevents the process from abolishing the conditions that make further correction possible? |

Closure without reopening becomes entrenchment. Reopening without closure
becomes endless contestation. Preservation without enforcement is a promise.
Enforcement without preservation can destroy the capacity it claims to protect.

The rules are a design checklist, not a constitution. They say what a correction
process has to answer before it can claim to be recursive.

## What corrigibility does not claim

Corrigibility is a necessary condition of democratic self-government. It is not
a complete account of justice or human flourishing. A correctable institution
can still distribute resources unjustly, impose unacceptable burdens, or
perpetuate harmful practices. Its amendability does not vindicate its present
arrangements.

The stronger formulation is therefore not that every institution must be
revisable. It is that an institution's claim to legitimacy depends partly on
whether those subject to its power possess effective, continuing, and
enforceable means to contest and revise the decisions, rules, and governing
arrangements that shape their lives. Those means must not merely exist at a
given moment. The institution's ordinary operation must reproduce the
capabilities, resources, and distributions of authority that make their future
exercise possible.

That is the move from corrigible institutions to the reproduction of democratic
self-government. It is also a research question, not a result: under what
conditions does an institution's ordinary operation increase or diminish the
capacity of those it governs to correct and transform it?
