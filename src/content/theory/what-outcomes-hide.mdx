Evaluation must account for the work behind a result and the authority of the people doing it.

## Same outcome, different burden

Two hospitals can produce identical recovery rates. Two schools can produce identical test scores. Two organizations can produce identical output. One gets there through robust systems. The other gets there through exhausted workers, family coordination, unofficial workarounds, and heroic discretion. The outcome measure sees where each arrived, not what people did on the way.

> An outcome tells you what was achieved, not what had to happen to people to achieve it.

Outcomes are not worthless. They are incomplete, and the gap is not random. What they omit is the cost the discipline exists to make visible.

## What a pure outcome orientation rewards

The incompleteness produces failures that compound.

**Outcomes can hide compensatory labor.** Identical results can depend on different amounts of unrecorded effort. A measure that omits that effort cannot distinguish [intrinsic performance](/glossary/intrinsic-performance) from [compensated performance](/glossary/compensated-performance). When people keep a bad rule from failing visibly, their repairs may be read as evidence that the rule works.

**Outcomes can reward cost displacement.** A benefits agency that cuts administrative cost by making applicants spend substantially more time documenting eligibility has the same accuracy and a better internal efficiency number. Labor was transferred. The outcome measure reads the transfer as improvement.

**Outcomes can conceal distributive asymmetry.** An intervention can improve average welfare while concentrating the cost of adapting on a small group. Aggregate results stay positive. The concentration is invisible unless the measure includes it.

**Outcomes do not capture authority.** Two institutions can produce identical outcomes while one lets people challenge, force reconsideration, alter rules, and obtain remedies, and the other merely happens to make good decisions. As long as results hold, an outcomes framework cannot tell them apart. But an institution that decides well and cannot be corrected is not the same as one that can. Today's good outcome does not guarantee tomorrow's. And people have a claim not only to be governed well but to hold standing against the power exercised over them.

**Outcomes erase counterfactual capacity.** Two systems can produce the same healthcare result while one requires the patient to spend a hundred hours navigating it and the other five. The endpoint is identical. The ninety-five hours are not. Those hours could have been rest, parenting, friendship, paid work, education, or nothing at all. Outcome measurement gives them no value. It cannot count time and attention that belonged to the person until the system took them.

**Outcomes cannot show whether the institution can learn.** A system can produce excellent results now and still be brittle. It may suppress dissent, depend on heroic workers, lack working appeals, ignore edge cases, or be unable to revise its categories. Corrigibility is not about whether the institution works today. It is about what happens when it stops working, and present outcomes cannot show that.

**Outcomes cannot settle legitimacy.** Pervasive surveillance might improve a desired outcome. Involuntary intervention might improve average health. Extreme employer control might raise productivity. An outcomes framework can fold side effects and rights into a complicated enough objective function. Once it does, outcomes are no longer doing the normative work. Prior judgments have decided which outcomes count, whose interests count, what cannot be traded away, and what authority an institution may hold. Effective does not imply legitimate. That is the [non-conversion principle](/research/theory/what-does-not-convert) applied to evaluation.

## What evaluation must include

None of this requires rejecting outcomes. An institution that is participatory, corrigible, and light on its people while failing at its purpose is not successful. Institutional evaluation needs at least three dimensions:

> **What did it accomplish? + What did accomplishing it require from people? + What power did those people have over those requirements?**

Or, compactly: outcome + adaptation burden + [corrective power](/glossary/corrective-power). The second term makes the [burden distribution](/evals/burden-concealment) visible rather than absorbed. The third term keeps [standing](/glossary/corrective-standing) in the measure, so that a system whose corrections are nominal is scored for what it is rather than for what it looks like.

The central evaluative question follows: **how far does the institution's apparent success depend on burdens it could reasonably eliminate?**

For a reform or a new system: does the deployment make compensating less necessary, or does it relocate, automate, or disguise the burden and leave people to absorb the friction?

> Never evaluate a system without evaluating what people must become, spend, absorb, and surrender for the system to produce its results.

The [design practice](/research/theory/ethotechnical-design) uses this assessment to decide which demands the institution should absorb.

## What the laws take from this

[Law IV](/standards/laws#law-iv) holds that every consequential delegation creates a correction obligation. That is the corrective-power dimension. A delegation scored on outcomes alone has hidden the correction capacity it owes.

[Law VII](/standards/laws#law-vii) gives error-bearing parties standing proportional to exposure. That is the question of power the outcome measure leaves out.

[Law VIII](/standards/laws#law-viii) holds that observability without state transition is theater. An outcome produced by absorption is that failure exactly: the number is observable, and nothing about it can change the rule that generated it.

[Law X](/standards/laws#law-x) puts the evaluation at the highest layer where harm can emerge. For adaptation burden, that is the household and the shift, not the organization's boundary.

The measurement point holds without the politics. An institution whose results depend on labor its measure cannot see will misread its own results, and it will not know what it is relying on until the labor stops.
