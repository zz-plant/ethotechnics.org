## The mirror objection

Every argument for standing meets a mirror argument. Grant exposed parties procedural force, the objection runs, and the institution drowns: every automated denial appealed by everyone, every halt triggered on suspicion, every decision reopened indefinitely. Public administration knows this worry in its concrete form. Permitting regimes, environmental review, and procurement are already criticized for veto-point proliferation, where each additional party with the power to pause makes the pausing permanent and nothing gets built. The objection deserves a serious answer, because it is not made in bad faith. Governance systems fail in two directions: through automated cruelty, and through paralysis. A method that only guards against the first produces the second.

Law VII already answers the narrow version of the objection. A challenge with procedural force must be received, answered, and able to change state; it need not prevail, and it does not stop the system on assertion. One challenge is a transaction with a cost, not a veto with an infinite one. What the law does not answer is the arithmetic that follows: one challenge is a transaction, but a hundred thousand challenges is a budget line, and no institutional design survives refusing to do arithmetic.

## Per-case force, aggregate risk

The aggregate failure has two forms, and they are different.

The first is volume in bad faith: challenges filed to exhaust, delay, or retaliate. The remedy for this exists and is unglamorous. Procedural force includes a standard of review and a set of admissible evidence, and a register can decline admissibility without declining standing. Filtering admissibility is a decision with a record; narrowing who may challenge is a change to the standing register that has to survive the same scrutiny as any other governance transition. The distinction matters because the first is a per-case call and the second is a constitutional one.

The second form is volume in good faith: a system that generates so many genuine errors that the people it exposes generate so many genuine challenges that the response apparatus saturates. Here the objection lands, and the honest reading of it is not that there are too many rights. It is that the system is producing too much harm. A queue of unanswered challenges is a measurement of the gap between the volume of error a system emits and the corrective capacity its delegation was granted with. Law IV states that no accumulation of delegated authority may proceed without a corresponding accumulation of corrective capacity. Challenge volume is where that law becomes countable.

## The cost of answering

Answering challenges is labor, and the labor has to be performed by someone. This is the symmetry the burden ledger already captures on one side and almost no institution records on the other. Unpaid waiting time and re-submission appear in burden accounting as costs pushed onto the exposed; unanswered appeals appear nowhere, because a queue that is aging invisibly is the system's own absorption mechanism. A challenge that sits past its clock is not pending; under Law VIII, silence past the clock is a governance failure, which means the institution is already recording the congestion as a failure state — it is simply not reading its own records as a capacity signal.

The economic incidence question in Law VII asks who pays for the repair. There is a mirror question about the challenge itself: who performs the answering, at what cost, and whether that cost was provisioned when the delegation was granted. A standing register that names responders and clocks has implicitly named a cost center. The institution that discovers it cannot afford its own register has not made an argument against standing; it has discovered the true size of the correction capacity behind its delegation, and the finding is informational. The response to that finding is resourcing or scope reduction — the two levers Law IV provides — not disqualification of the people the system fails.

## What congestion actually means

When challenge volume exceeds response capacity, institutions reach for the lever closest to hand, and it is almost always narrowing: fewer parties eligible to challenge, narrower challengeable matter, higher admissibility bars, longer clocks. Each narrowing is individually defensible and collectively the whole failure. Narrowing standing in response to congestion converts the open circuit back into the closed one. The people the system fails lose the instrument exactly when the volume proves the system is failing often. The signal that something is wrong arrives through volume, and the response destroys the channel that delivered it.

Read correctly, congestion means something specific: the density of challenges is a rate, and a rate can be governed by capacity decisions rather than by rights restrictions. An institution treats challenge density as a governing signal when it ties sustained breaches of response clocks to a resourcing decision or a scope decision, and records which one was made. An institution treats it as a nuisance when it ties the same breaches to eligibility criteria. The two responses can look identical in a quarter of calm and diverge completely in a year of scale.

## Stratification

The other lever operates on the force side rather than the volume side. Not every challenge should carry the same halt potential, and not every halt should be available at the same tier. A frontline operator pausing one case, a supervisor halting a queue, and a steward stopping a system are different instruments with different blast radii, different restoration paths, and different evidence demands. Stratifying halt authority by blast radius is what keeps per-case force from becoming system-wide friction: the power to stop scales with the size of what is being stopped, and the smallest tiers stay cheap enough to use that people actually use them.

This is also the answer to the vetocracy worry in its institutional form. Veto-point proliferation is what happens when every party holds the same maximal instrument — the full stop — and no one holds a graduated one. A halt structure with tiers does not remove veto points; it prices them. The question about any proposed standing grant stops being "can this paralyze us" and becomes "which tier does this force operate at, and is the restoration path provisioned at that tier."

## What the laws take from this

The method takes from this argument three commitments and leaves the rest as motivation. Correction capacity is measured against challenge volume, not asserted alongside it — the challenge load ledger is the instrument, and sustained congestion is a capacity event, not an eligibility event. Halt force is stratified by blast radius, so that the power to stop scales with the size of what is being stopped. And narrowing standing in response to volume is a named failure mode: an institution that does it has treated a capacity signal as a permissions problem, and its records should say so.

A reader can accept the vetocracy objection in full and still accept these commitments. They do not grant more power to anyone. They require that the cost of the power already granted be counted, provisioned, and recorded — which is the arithmetic the objection was asking for all along.
