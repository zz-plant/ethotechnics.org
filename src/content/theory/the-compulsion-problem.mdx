## The question the method cannot answer for itself

A standard binds whoever adopts it. Nothing about a standard compels adoption. That is the enforcement problem. This site's [method](/method) asks institutions to keep the evidence behind each decision, answer challenges by a deadline, name who may challenge, and give someone the power to stop the system. Each of these asks the institution to constrain itself. The institutions that most need the constraint are the least likely to want it. The state is often the deployer, the procurement customer, and the beneficiary of automation that pushes costs onto others, all at once. Asking it to bind itself is asking the party that gains from the arrangement to end it.

The honest answer is that the method has no lever of its own. Compulsion always comes from somewhere else. That fact sets a design constraint: every clause must be adoptable by an institution that does not want to be good, because someone outside the institution can make adoption cheaper than the alternative.

## Where binding force comes from

The forces that compel institutions, which this essay calls vectors, are few, old, and outside any standards body.

Procurement compels through contract. A buyer who writes requirements into a vendor agreement obtains what legislation cannot reach across a vendor boundary: an enforceable obligation, with damages, against a counterparty that wanted the deal. The method publishes [contract language for buyers](/standards/where-this-binds) because contract is the fastest compulsion available and the least dependent on the buyer's virtue. It requires only that one party to the transaction have leverage, which is far easier to arrange than an act of a legislature.

Courts compel through discovery and procedure. A decision record that shows what evidence the system acted on, what would have invalidated it, and who was authorized is exactly the artifact litigation extracts from institutions anyway. The method's objects are shaped by what a subpoena can do with them. An institution that maintains the record honestly has a defense; an institution that cannot produce the record has an admission. Either way, the record's existence changes the cost of contestation for the exposed party, which is the vector doing the work.

Liability pricing compels through insurance and audit markets. Underwriters and attestation firms price what they can inspect. A deployment that records how long a halt takes, how many hours of burden fall on people, and whether repairs were made is inspectable in a way that a policy document is not, and the differential reaches an institution as a premium or an audit finding without anyone choosing to be governed.

Regulators compel through safe harbor. A regulator that cannot write detailed rules can still recognize a standard: adoption becomes evidence of diligence, and evidence of diligence is worth having the day something goes wrong. This vector requires the standard to be specific enough to audit against, which is why the method publishes clauses with evidence requirements rather than principles with adjectives. The recognition has to run one way: adoption can count as evidence of diligence, but conformance is never evidence that the power itself should be held, and it does not extinguish anyone's statutory rights or the operator's liability. Otherwise the standard becomes a way to launder the decision it was meant to expose.

Workers compel through standing, a recognized right to challenge, and its legal analogues. Frontline operators who can halt a system without being punished for it are an enforcement mechanism inside the deployment; labor law, whistleblower protection, and collective agreement are its external forms. The model is the andon cord, which any worker on a Toyota assembly line can pull to stop the line: a claim on the institution that someone other than the institution can pull.

Public memory compels by resisting drift. Institutions re-litigate their commitments as staff turn over. That is why the method's language is anchored to stable glossary permalinks and versioned standards. A public artifact with a date defeats the argument that the commitment was never made.

## What this means for the writing of clauses

Each vector imposes a design constraint on the method, and they converge on one property: a clause has to be worth adopting to an institution that does not want it. A clause that only an institution that wants to be good would adopt is decoration. The test for every clause is whether some holder of one of the six vectors would pay for it — a buyer in a contract, a defendant in discovery, an underwriter pricing a policy, a regulator recognizing diligence, a worker pulling the cord, a journalist reading a dated record.

That test explains features of the method that look like editorial choices. Clauses are written as testable invariants because audit markets and regulators need something to check. Standards carry evidence packs because discovery and attestation need artifacts. Mechanisms name owners and clocks because contract remedies need breach conditions. Every requirement is time-bound because a commitment without a clock cannot be breached, and what cannot be breached cannot compel.

## The objection from the neutral partner

One more objection belongs here, because it is not about whether the claims are true. It is about what it costs a profession to say them. Human factors, cognitive systems engineering, and safety science work inside the institutions they study, and their access is bought with a particular stance: the neutral partner who improves the joint system, not the critic who indicts it. That stance bought the fields their seats in aviation, medicine, and defense. Safety science distinguishes work-as-imagined, how procedures say work happens, from work-as-done, how it actually gets done. Call the gap between them an extractive labor relation and the seat is at risk, because a discipline that arrives as a prosecutor is shown the door, and the door is where the observations are made.

The objection is not made in bad faith, and the method should say so plainly. The practitioner who gets into the room observes more than the one refused entry. Much of the gap is unexamined rather than designed. The neutral framing is not a false description of the world; it is a bet about what change costs.

The answer has two parts. The first is the argument of this essay: the instruments do not require the politics to function. A [rescue register](/mechanisms/patterns/rescue-register) that logs each failure a person caught, a [burden dashboard](/mechanisms/patterns/burden-dashboards) that counts the hours people spend coping, a requirement that each claim be tied to its evidence — each can enter a deployment as a procurement requirement from a buyer with leverage, or as a telemetry proposal from a practitioner, with no mention of extraction anywhere. What compels is the contract and the clock, and neither cares what the person who wrote them believes.

The second part concedes the price. Neutrality limits what can be asked for. A discipline that cannot name the gap as a cost cannot request the records that would price it, because the request carries the diagnosis, and a diagnosis of extraction is expensive for the institution that would have to act on it. Framed as an indictment, the request is refused. Framed as telemetry, the same request is often accepted. That is a claim about incentives, not motives. The fields' own record bears it out: access has produced safety learning without cost accounting. The near-miss is recorded and the labor it took to catch it is not. Recording the cost is an obligation, and an obligation needs one of the vectors above. Persuasion is not one of them.

The trade between access and saying what one sees is real. It is a judgment, not a measurement, and nothing on this site settles it. What the method claims is narrower: the records are neutral, the obligation structure that makes them binding does not care who asks for them, and the practitioner who can obtain the observation while writing the obligation into the requirements is using both channels at once.

## The state binding itself

The hardest case remains: the state that is deployer, customer, and beneficiary. No external vector reaches it cleanly. It writes the procurement, it runs the courts, and its regulator answers to its treasury. But the mechanism by which any institution binds itself is not a mystery, and the state invented it. Constitutional constraint is a decision recorded as a rule about future decisions, with the change of rule made harder to take than the ordinary decision it governs. The method applies that mechanism to each deployment: authority is a grant with a recorded basis, an expiry, and conditions for revision, not a possession.

Applied to itself, the claim is symmetrical. A legislature that adopts the standing register for its own automated systems has made the same move any adopter makes: recorded who may challenge what, with what force, answered by whom. The state does not need to be virtuous to do this. It needs the same thing every adopter needs — a vector — and for the state the vectors are the ones constitutionalism already built: judicial review, ombudsman offices, legislative oversight, and the standing of citizens to sue. Those institutions predate the method and will outlast it; the method's contribution is to make their inputs specific, inspectable, and cheap to exercise at the point where a formal system meets what it cannot represent.

## What the laws take from this

The method takes one commitment from this argument and leaves the rest as strategy. Adoption paths are part of the method, not an afterthought. A clause that cannot be stated in [procurement language](/standards/std-02-contestability-recourse), discovered in litigation, priced by an underwriter, or recognized by a regulator is not finished. Where a standard has no adopter, it says so rather than implying force it does not have, and the registry records the difference between published and adopted.

The laws the essay cites are the ones that make a commitment breachable. [Law I](/standards/laws#law-i) makes authority a recorded grant, not a fact of possession, so there is something for a contract or a court to read. [Law II](/standards/laws#law-ii) puts an expiry on the grant, so the commitment has a clock and can be breached. [Law XII](/standards/laws#law-xii) protects the capacities a challenge depends on, including the worker's halt and the dated public record.

Who should hold power over institutions is a political argument, and the method claims no jurisdiction over it. Its claim is narrower and checkable. Binding force is always external, it arrives through six vectors with known requirements, and a standard written to those requirements can move an institution its author cannot compel.
