title: "Capacity planning for consequential decisions"
description: "The queueing model behind the invariant: the arrival rate of consequential decisions against the rate of valid verification, and the four levers that restore parity."
permalink: "/explainers/capacity-planning"
eyebrow: "Explainer"
glossaryRef: "verification-fidelity"
definition:
  - "The invariant says no system may accumulate consequential agency faster than the institution accumulates the capacity to inspect, challenge, revise, and survive its decisions. As a first approximation, that is a queue: consequential decisions arrive at rate λ, and valid verification is served at rate μ."
  - "The ratio Φ = λ / (μ × q) is the load on the verification system. q is fidelity: the share of inspections that actually reconstruct the decision and can answer what would have changed the outcome. When Φ exceeds 1, uninspected decisions accumulate. The institution can let them through, which turns sign-off into rubber-stamping, or accumulate unremediated error until an external shock halts the system."
  - "The answer is not always more reviewers. The four levers are scope, autonomy, volume, and verification. Narrow what the system decides, lower its authority, reduce the number of consequential decisions, or raise the rate and fidelity of verification. An institution that pulls none of them and adds paperwork has renamed the gap."
example: "Illustrative: a prior-authorization queue receives 1,200 consequential decisions a day. Forty reviewers complete thirty valid reviews each, and sampling puts fidelity at 0.75. Φ = 1,200 / (40 × 30 × 0.75) = 1.33, so the queue grows. The institution narrows the automatic-denial class to 800 decisions a day and raises fidelity to 0.85. Φ = 800 / (40 × 30 × 0.85) = 0.78. The numbers are invented to show the arithmetic; the point is that narrowing the delegation is a legitimate way to restore parity."
distinctSignals:
  - title: "Arrival rate of consequential decisions"
    detail: "Count state changes that bind someone, not every model output. A ranking that a person must act on is a different arrival rate from an automatic denial."
  - title: "Valid verification rate"
    detail: "Count reviews that can reconstruct the decision, not sign-offs. A reviewer approving 400 cases an hour is producing throughput, not verification."
  - title: "Fidelity"
    detail: "Sample the decisions and test whether a reviewer can answer what would have changed the outcome. A rubber stamp has high throughput and near-zero fidelity."
  - title: "Queue age"
    detail: "Report the age of the oldest uninspected consequential decision. A stable average can hide a tail that never gets reviewed."
  - title: "Which lever moved"
    detail: "When Φ exceeds 1, record whether scope, autonomy, volume, or verification changed. If none did, the institution chose to run unaudited."

faqs:
  - question: "Is this Little's Law?"
    answer: "It borrows the queue logic, not the steady-state assumptions. Institutional arrivals are bursty and verification is heterogeneous, so treat Φ as a first-order load signal, not a precise forecast."
  - question: "Does the invariant require linear hiring?"
    answer: "No. It requires capacity proportional to consequential agency. Scope, autonomy, and volume can restore the ratio without hiring, and verification can be raised by tooling as well as by staff."
  - question: "How is fidelity measured?"
    answer: "Sample the decisions and test whether a reviewer can reconstruct what would have changed the outcome. The reconstructability test in MEC-29 is the unit; a fluent post-hoc explanation is not enough."
  - question: "What happens when Φ exceeds 1?"
    answer: "The institution chooses a lever or accepts that sign-off is decorative. Letting the queue grow is a choice to run unaudited, even if no one records it as one."
