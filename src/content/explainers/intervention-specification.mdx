title: "What is an intervention specification?"
description: "An intervention specification replaces “human in the loop” with six answerable questions about who can change the system."
permalink: "/explainers/intervention-specification"
eyebrow: "Explainer"
glossaryRef: "intervention-specification"
definition:
  - "An intervention specification states six things: who may intervene, on what signal, with what information, with authority over which part of the system's state, how fast, and what happens when they disagree. Every oversight clause should come down to one."
  - "Ethotechnics retires “human in the loop” as the name of a control, because a person can be named in the loop and still be unable to change anything."
example: "A payments hold queue names two on-call reviewers. It names the fraud-score signal that routes cases to them, the customer history they see, their authority to release or freeze the account, a four-minute budget per case, and the route to the duty manager when they disagree with the model."
distinctSignals:

    detail: "The specification names something the reviewer can change, not only a decision they can approve. The route for disagreeing has been used at least once in the last quarter."
  - title: "Oversight as a role and a meeting"
    detail: "Oversight is specified as a role and a meeting. The reviewer sees a score without the case behind it, cannot change a policy, and has no set route when they disagree."

faqs:
  - question: "Why retire “human in the loop”?"
    answer: "Because it names a position, not a capability. A person can be in the loop and still lack the information, authority, time, or permission to disagree. Then they are not part of the control system."
  - question: "Does every automated step need one?"
    answer: "No. It is needed wherever someone claims oversight. A step with no oversight claimed needs no specification, but an assurance document cannot then claim oversight for it."
