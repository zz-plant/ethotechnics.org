title: "Democratic vs. coercive governability"
description: "Governability can either protect rights through contestability or enforce control without standing or remedy."
permalink: "/explainers/democratic-vs-coercive-governability"
eyebrow: "Explainer"
glossaryRef: "democratic-vs-coercive-governability"
definition:
  - "Democratic governability is infrastructure that expands standing, enables contestation, and guarantees remedies when harm occurs."
  - "Coercive governability is control infrastructure that optimizes for compliance and extraction without enforceable rights, independent oversight, or repair obligations."
example: "A democratic benefits platform provides appeal routes, repair SLAs, and human escalation. A coercive counterpart offers one-way notices, no reversal path, and opaque denial tooling."
distinctSignals:
  - title: "Governance test"
    detail: "Compare whether people can refuse, negotiate, or appeal automation versus being forced through single-path compliance."
  - title: "Common failure pattern"
    detail: "Dashboards can look participatory while defaults remain coercive; audit opt-out friction and penalty structures directly."
  - title: "Evidence to ship"
    detail: "Publish participation metrics, policy change logs from public feedback, and examples where dissent changed system behavior."

faqs:
  - question: "What separates democratic governability from compliance theater?"
    answer: "Democratic governability gives affected parties standing, binds timelines to remedy, and changes outcomes when harm is confirmed."
  - question: "How do you detect coercive governability early?"
    answer: "Look for decision systems that log disputes but never change outcomes, rely on delay, or restrict escalation to internal-only channels."
  - question: "Which metrics make this measurable?"
    answer: "Track appeal passage rates, time-to-restore, time-in-harm, and the share of decisions that can be reversed without executive override."
