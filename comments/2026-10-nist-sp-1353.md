# Public comment on NIST SP 1353 ipd

**Document:** NIST SP 1353 ipd — *QuickStart Guide for Using Artificial Intelligence (AI) for Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 Analysis and Reporting*
**Comment deadline:** 15 October 2026, 11:59 PM
**Submit to:** csf@nist.gov
**Status:** DRAFT — not sent. See "Before sending" at the bottom.

## Summary

SP 1353 ipd describes using AI to analyze cybersecurity policy, build organizational
profiles, and develop target states. Each of those outputs is an assurance artifact:
a document that an organization, an auditor, or a regulator later reads as evidence
that governance happened.

The draft treats the quality of those artifacts as the thing to get right. This comment
argues that artifact quality is the wrong control point, and that a guide which
accelerates artifact production without addressing artifact *standing* will predictably
degrade the assurance value of the CSF profile — not because the AI is inaccurate, but
because provenance, contestability, and stopping authority are not properties the
artifact currently carries.

Three specific recommendations follow. All three are additive and none require changing
the CSF itself.

## 1. An AI-generated profile and a human-authored profile are not interchangeable evidence, and the document should not let them look identical

The draft's three use cases — policy evaluation, organizational profiling, and target
state development — all produce output in the same format a human analyst would produce.
Downstream, nothing distinguishes them.

This matters because a CSF profile is consumed as an assertion about an organization,
and the weight a reader gives it depends on who stands behind it. When an AI system
drafts a Current Profile and a human approves it in bulk, the resulting document asserts
the same thing as one built from interviews, but far less is known about how it came to
say what it says.

**Recommendation.** SP 1353 should direct that any CSF artifact produced with AI
assistance carry, inline and machine-readably: the model and version used, the inputs it
was given, which portions were human-verified versus accepted as generated, and the name
of the person accountable for the assertion. Not an appendix — a property of each claim.

This is what the Ethotechnics framework calls an evidence state: the artifact records
what is known about its own basis, so a later reader can tell what they are relying on.

## 2. Automating the analysis loop can accelerate the appearance of improvement without changing the capacity to respond

CSF 2.0's Improve (ID.IM) function assumes an organization learns between cycles. When
profiling and target-state development are automated, cycle time drops sharply. The
number of documented improvement cycles rises. Whether the organization can actually
detect, stop, or recover from an incident may not move at all.

This is the failure mode already documented against ISO/IEC 42001: a management system
can certify sustained governance effort while the underlying system remains difficult to
halt mid-incident. Effort is easy to evidence. Interruption capacity is not, so it gets
measured last or not at all.

**Recommendation.** SP 1353 should state plainly that a shortened analysis cycle is not
evidence of improved posture, and should recommend that organizations using AI for CSF
analysis pair each cycle with at least one measure that cannot be produced by the
analysis itself — a tested rollback, a live tabletop, a measured detection or containment
time. If the only thing that improved is the reporting, the guide should say that is a
null result.

## 3. Give the subjects of an automated profile a way to contest it

A Current Profile makes findings about teams, systems, and people. Where AI generates
those findings at scale, the people characterized by them typically learn the outcome
and not the basis, and have no defined route to challenge a finding they believe is wrong.

An incorrect finding that cannot be corrected does not stay contained. It propagates into
the target state, the remediation plan, and the budget built on top of them.

**Recommendation.** SP 1353 should include a short contestability baseline for
AI-generated findings: who has standing to challenge a finding, what reasons they are
owed, what record is kept, what the response timeline is, and what happens to
downstream artifacts when a finding is overturned. A one-page version sufficient for this
purpose is published as MVC-01 (Minimum Viable Contestability) at
https://ethotechnics.org/standards — it is offered freely and NIST is welcome to adapt
or ignore the specific text; the recommendation is that *some* such baseline appear.

## On the framework referenced above

Ethotechnics is an open framework for the operational accountability of high-stakes
automated systems, published at https://ethotechnics.org under CC BY-SA 4.0. It treats
evidence, authorization, challenge, reconsideration, correction, and escalation as
explicit states of a deployed system rather than as policy commitments about it. The
standards referenced here are MVC-01 (Minimum Viable Contestability, stable) and STD-02
(Contestability & Recourse, draft). I maintain it and have no commercial interest in its
adoption.
