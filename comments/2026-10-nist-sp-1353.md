**Document:** NIST SP 1353 ipd — _NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: Quick-Start

(August 2026, DOI 10.6028/NIST.SP.1353.ipd)
**Comment deadline:** 15 October 2026, 11:59 PM
**Submit to:** csf@nist.gov
**Status:** DRAFT — reviewed against the full ipd; not yet sent. See "Before

## Summary

SP 1353 ipd shows organizations how to use generative AI to produce three CSF
2.0 assurance artifacts: a governance alignment review (Use Case 1), a Current
State Profile (Use Case 2), and a Target State Profile (Use Case 3). The guide
already does several things right that similar guidance omits: the sample
prompts demand source-grounding ("Use only the attached source materials"; "No
fabrication. If an outcome is not addressed in the sources, say so plainly" —
Use Case 2, p. 5), the supplemental corpus is explicitly fictitious (Appendix
A, p. 9), and the Important Notes require qualified human review before any
output reaches organizational decision-making (p. 3).

This comment argues that the guide stops one step short in three places, and
that each gap matters more here than it would in ordinary guidance, because
Appendix A's workflow is copy-paste by design (p. 9: "Copy the entire CO-STAR
formatted prompt … and paste into the AI tool"). Whatever a quick-start guide
models is what practitioners will ship.

## 1. Provenance appears in the prompts and the reminders — it should be a required property of the artifact

The draft gestures at provenance three times: the Use Case 2 prompt asks for an
"Assumptions & Evidence Gaps" note and Strength/Partial/Gap prefixes (p. 5);
the Reference Data panel says AI-assisted mappings "should retain identifiers,
source context, provenance, and statuses" (p. 7); and the glossary defines
"Proposed / Derived mapping" as a status label pending expert validation
(p. 8).

What none of these reach: the produced Profile itself. A Current State Profile
drafted by a model and approved in bulk asserts the same thing as one built
from interviews, and nothing in the artifact distinguishes them afterward. The
guide's own review requirement (p. 3) is unauditable a year later unless the
artifact records that the review happened, and of what.

**Recommendation.** Add to "Tips for Getting Started" (p. 7) that any CSF
artifact produced with AI assistance carry, in the artifact: the model and
version used, the source-material set it was given, which rows were
human-verified versus accepted as generated, and the accountable reviewer. The
guide's advice to compare multiple AI tools (pp. 3, 7) makes this concrete: the
comparison is only meaningful later if the adopted artifact records which
tool's output it adopted. This extends the p. 7 mapping-provenance language
from crosswalks to the Profiles themselves.

## 2. The speed the guide promises can masquerade as the improvement CSF measures

Use Case 2 promises "compressing the initial drafting from weeks to hours"
(p. 5); Use Case 3 promises reduced "manual research and writing time" from
blank page to workable draft (p. 6). Both claims are credible. Neither is an
improvement in security posture — but both produce the artifacts by which
posture improvement is evidenced under the CSF's Improvement outcomes. An
organization that automates its analysis loop will show more documented cycles
at higher polish while detection, containment, and recovery stay exactly where
they were.

**Recommendation.** One sentence in "Putting It Into Practice" (p. 7): a
shortened analysis cycle is not evidence of improved posture, and organizations
using AI for CSF analysis should pair each cycle with at least one measure the
analysis itself cannot generate — a tested recovery, a tabletop, a measured
detection or containment time. The guide already warns that AI output can be
wrong (Hallucination, p. 8); this warns that it can be right and still change
nothing.

## 3. Findings about people need a challenge route, and Use Case 2 generates them at scale

Use Case 2 maps "personnel interview notes" to CSF outcomes and records
"observed gaps in the interviews" (p. 2), graded Strength/Partial/Gap (p. 5).
These are findings about named teams' practices, produced by a model, feeding
gap analysis and prioritization — and eventually budgets. The guide requires
review by qualified personnel (p. 3), but review by the artifact's consumers is
not recourse for its subjects: a team characterized as a "Gap" has no defined
route to contest the finding, and an uncorrected wrong finding propagates into
the Target Profile and the roadmap built on it.

**Recommendation.** Add a step to the stakeholder-convening tip (p. 7): before
ingesting interview notes, define who may challenge a finding, what reasons
they are owed, what record is kept, and what happens to downstream artifacts
when a finding is overturned. A one-page baseline sufficient for this purpose
is published as MVC-01 (Minimum Viable Contestability) at
https://ethotechnics.org/standards — offered freely; the recommendation is
that some such baseline appear, not that this one be adopted.

## On the framework referenced above

Ethotechnics is an open framework for the operational accountability of
high-stakes automated systems, published at https://ethotechnics.org under
CC BY-SA 4.0. It treats evidence, authorization, challenge, reconsideration,
correction, and escalation as explicit states of a deployed system rather than
as policy commitments about it. I maintain it and have no commercial interest
in its adoption.
